NASA's Webb Captures Commotion From Nebula's Stellar Jets

NASA Science

The James Webb Space Telescope peered into NGC 7129, a stellar nursery about 3,300 light-years away, and found a crowd of young stars that dust had kept hidden from earlier telescopes. Newborn protostars are firing narrow jets of gas outward, carving and stirring the cloud they were born from.

You're looking at stars in the act of shaping their own birthplace.

Physicists just saw quarks make waves in the Big Bang's primordial soup

ScienceDaily

At CERN, physicists recreated quark-gluon plasma, the super-hot matter that filled the universe for a split second after the Big Bang, and watched quarks send ripples through it. The plasma didn't behave like a loose gas of particles. It slowed them down and reacted as a whole, like a liquid.

The universe's first "ocean" seems to have been a very smooth liquid.

Stem cells survive unexpectedly rough space travel

Phys.org

Clusters of neural stem cells went to the International Space Station in 2024. Bad landing weather then delayed their return, so they spent far longer than planned without proper life support. Some survived anyway and could still turn into working nerve and glial cells, and the ones grown in 3D-printed scaffolds held up best.

One day astronauts might grow repair tissue right where they are.

Batizada de 'Maria', nova espécie de anfíbio homenageia o nome mais popular do Brasil

Agência FAPESP

Uma cobra-cega que só existe no estado do Rio ganhou o nome Siphonops maria. O nome homenageia as milhões de Marias do país e também Maria, uma das 11 pessoas escravizadas que plantaram mais de 100 mil árvores na Floresta da Tijuca entre 1861 e 1874. Com DNA e tomografia, os pesquisadores viram que exemplares guardados em museus desde 1902 já eram da nova espécie, mas tinham sido confundidos com uma parente.

Uma espécie nova que estava na gaveta havia mais de um século.

Artificial nests give Hong Kong's cockatoos a home – and a chance to study their behavior

Mongabay

About 200 critically endangered yellow-crested cockatoos live in Hong Kong. They probably descend from escaped pets, and they now make up around 10% of the world's wild population. A biologist and an arborist have put up 15 nest boxes in the city, and four already have breeding pairs. In one case a chick was rescued from an air-conditioning vent and placed in a box, and its parents found it within seven hours.

A city of skyscrapers has become an ark for a bird that is vanishing at home.

The women putting community at the heart of elephant conservation

Positive News

Reteti, in northern Kenya, is Africa's first Indigenous-owned elephant sanctuary, and many of its staff are women. One of them is Dorothy Lowakutuk, among the continent's first female elephant keepers. The sanctuary rescues orphaned calves and returns them to the wild. Its goat milk comes from 1,250 local "milk mamas", who were paid $342,000 last year. The region's elephants have grown from a few hundred 30 years ago to more than 6,000.

Conservation works best when the neighbours are partners.

Remarkable fossil of a feathered, winged dinosaur hints that flight evolved multiple times

Smithsonian Magazine

A new Microraptor fossil shows that this small, four-winged dinosaur picked up its flight features in a different order than modern birds did. That supports the idea that dinosaurs worked out how to fly more than once, along separate evolutionary paths.

Evolution may have invented flight several times before birds made it stick.

Archaeologists may have finally solved a 100-year-old Florida mystery

ScienceDaily

For about a century, archaeologists couldn't explain why pottery made in the Mississippian style turned up around Tampa Bay. New analysis suggests that potters moved south and brought the techniques with them. They also mixed crushed pieces of older, ancestral pots into the new clay.

Grinding grandma's pottery into your own is a lovely way to carry the past forward.

Through Fictional Self-Portraits, Tang Shuo Visualizes an Ancestral Archive

Colossal

Tang Shuo's village of Dashishan in southern China has shrunk from more than 200 people in his childhood to about a dozen. He paints himself into the roles his ancestors may have had, in oil on linen with lush forests and candy-coloured clouds that tie the generations together. His first New York solo show, Borrowed History, opens on October 8.
