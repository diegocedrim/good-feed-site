Rochas da Paraíba confirmam que o campo magnético da Terra estava fraco durante a explosão da vida

Jornal da USP

Há uns 525 milhões de anos, logo depois da explosão cambriana, quando os primeiros animais se diversificavam, o escudo magnético da Terra estava surpreendentemente fraco. Uma equipe do IAG-USP, liderada por Ricardo Trindade, e da Universidade de Liverpool mediu o magnetismo gravado em diques vulcânicos de Itabaiana, na Paraíba, e em rochas da Nova Escócia, no Canadá.

Os valores ficaram entre 8 e 12 microteslas na Paraíba e abaixo de 1 no Canadá. Hoje, o campo fica entre 25 e 65. Esse período fraco durou cerca de 40 milhões de anos, talvez enquanto o núcleo interno do planeta ainda se cristalizava. O estudo saiu na Science Advances.

Uma pedra do Nordeste guardou, como uma bússola fóssil, o estado do coração do planeta quando a vida animal estava começando.

NASA's Hubble Spies Spiral's Edge

NASA Science

NGC 1827 is a spiral galaxy about 37 million light-years away in the constellation Columba, the Dove, and we see it almost exactly edge-on. Hubble's view shows a thin blade of blue star-forming regions cut by dark lanes of dust. A foreground star in our own galaxy sits near its center, its diffraction spikes making a little cross.

The image comes from a survey of 260 small satellite galaxies around 22 Milky Way-like hosts. Astronomers want to know whether our models of the universe hold up at those small scales.

Solving the gallium anomaly: a neutrino mystery may not need new particles after all

Phys.org

For decades, gallium detectors caught about 20% fewer electron neutrinos than theory predicted. Many physicists blamed a hypothetical "sterile neutrino", but that explanation never agreed with other experiments. Matteo Cadeddu, of Italy's National Institute for Nuclear Physics, went back to first principles. He treated the electron and the nucleus together, relativistically, using the Dirac equation instead of handling them separately.

Solved numerically, the corrected prediction lines up with the measurements. Published in Physical Review Letters, the work grew out of a problem Cadeddu first tackled in his master's thesis. His co-authors include his former students and his wife, physicist Francesca Dordei.

Sometimes the exotic new particle turns out to be a calculation done more carefully.

Whales take different routes but still reach dinner on time

Phys.org

University of Auckland researchers tagged 21 southern right whales, or tohorā, leaving New Zealand's subantarctic Auckland Islands for feeding grounds south of Australia. About half swam fairly directly west. The rest took a long L-shaped detour far to the south, which averaged 64 days against 42.

The detour group left about a month earlier and swam more slowly, so both groups arrived at about the same time. One female switched routes depending on whether she had a calf with her. The whales seem to navigate using currents, seafloor shape and the magnetic field together.

Bancos de rodolitos abrigam cerca de 1% da vida marinha na costa brasileira

Jornal da USP

Rodolitos são "pedrinhas" vivas: algas vermelhas que depositam calcário em camadas, como uma cebola, e ficam soltas no fundo do mar. Pesquisas sobre os bancos de Abrolhos, na Bahia, e da Ilha da Queimada Grande, em São Paulo, estimam que esses tapetes abrigam cerca de 1% da vida marinha da costa brasileira.

Entre as bolinhas se escondem caranguejos, peixes e outras algas, e isso atrai predadores maiores. Os bancos ainda ajudam no ciclo do carbono e continuam pouco estudados.

Um recife que rola: um dos ecossistemas mais discretos e importantes do nosso litoral.

Seagrass meadows may offer coastal communities a more nutritious catch than reefs

Mongabay

Coral reefs get the glory, but along the coasts of Kenya, Tanzania and Mozambique, the humble seagrass meadow may matter more for dinner. A study in Cell Reports Sustainability measured six key nutrients in local catches, including iron, zinc, selenium, vitamin A and omega-3s. By that measure, seagrass fisheries were 1.6 times more "nutritionally multifunctional" than reefs.

More than 3.5 million people in the region depend on these fisheries. For them, a reliably nourishing catch can matter as much as a big one.

Só Notícia Boa

Nas Minas Gerais escravista do século 19, negros e mulheres ensinavam as primeiras letras

Jornal da USP

Cruzando censos, jornais de época e petições de professores, a pesquisadora Vanessa Souza Batista, da Faculdade de Educação da USP, localizou centenas de mestres de primeiras letras em Minas Gerais nas décadas de 1820 e 1830. Cerca de 37% deles eram negros.

Entre eles havia pelo menos quatro homens libertos que davam aulas particulares. Também havia mulheres solteiras, chefes de família, que se sustentavam ensinando a ler. É uma história da educação brasileira que estava escondida nos arquivos.

Uma homenagem atrasada, um dia depois do Dia do Professor, a professores que a história quase esqueceu.

Teen's messaging app can "hop" across Bluetooth when cell networks go down

Good News Network

Emirhan Durham, 17, from Istanbul, built Crisis Connect, an app that keeps people in touch when cell towers fail. Each phone becomes a relay, and messages and calls hop from device to device over Bluetooth until they reach the person they're meant for. No central server is needed.

It's free and open source, with SOS beacons, live location and a digital whistle. Durham is clear it won't replace emergency radio. It's meant to fill the gap in the first hours, when phones are all people have.

The idea treats the millions of phones already in people's pockets as a network that just needs switching on.

It's official: timekeepers have gotten rid of the leap second and introduced the leap hour

Smithsonian Magazine

Earth's spin isn't perfectly steady, so since 1972 timekeepers have slipped 27 extra seconds into the world's official clock to keep it in step with the planet. Those surprise seconds have long been a headache for computers and networks.

Now the leap second is officially retired. Clock time and Earth's rotation will be allowed to drift apart, and the gap will someday be closed with a single "leap hour". At the current pace, that probably won't happen for a very long time.

A monumental 18th-century portrait of a young Polynesian man visits the U.S. for the first time

Smithsonian Magazine

Mai, a young man from Ra'iātea, near Tahiti, sailed to Britain with James Cook's expedition. He became the first Pacific Islander to visit the country, and something of a celebrity in 1770s London. Joshua Reynolds painted him life-size, barefoot and robed in white, and the painting is often called one of the great portraits of its century.

Now the "Portrait of Mai" is crossing the Atlantic for its first-ever showing in the United States.

'Radiant Geometries' illuminates teachings and technologies in the Indigenous Americas

Colossal

At Rice University's Moody Center for the Arts in Houston, through December 19, more than 200 works by 50 Indigenous artists from across the Americas explore ancestral knowledge about land, water and caring for other species. There are vast geometric paintings by Eamon Ore-Giron, inspired by medicine wheels and Amazonian textiles. Diné weaver Melissa Cody combines traditional patterns with a Jacquard loom.

The show also includes feathered "emergency totems" by the Aymara artist Natalia Montoya Lecaros and a sound installation about where two rivers meet.

Geometry turns out to be a language that's very old and still in use.

16 de outubro de 1846: a primeira cirurgia sem dor

Wikipédia

Há exatos 180 anos, num anfiteatro do Hospital Geral de Massachusetts, em Boston, o dentista William Morton fez um paciente inalar vapor de éter. Em seguida, o cirurgião John Collins Warren retirou um tumor do pescoço dele sem que ele gritasse. Foi a primeira demonstração pública da anestesia cirúrgica. "Senhores, isto não é embuste", teria dito Warren à plateia. A sala ficou conhecida como Ether Dome e existe até hoje.

Arthur Conan Doyle based Sherlock Holmes on a real-life Scottish surgeon

Smithsonian Magazine

Before Baker Street there was Edinburgh, where a young medical student named Arthur Conan Doyle learned from Joseph Bell. Bell was a surgeon and lecturer who could famously read a patient's job, travels and habits from their hands, walk and accent before they said a word.

Drawing on a new biography, this Smithsonian history feature shows how Bell's habit of close observation changed medical diagnosis, how police sometimes asked for his help, and how it all ended up in the world's most famous fictional detective. A nice one for the weekend.
