Do spiders dream? Scientists just found a surprising clue

ScienceDaily

Researchers at the University of Bonn filmed Portia fimbriata, a coin-sized Australian jumping spider, with infrared cameras over several days and nights. They found two distinct sleep stages, including one with twitching legs and eye movements that looks a lot like our REM sleep. When the spiders were kept awake, they slept more afterwards, just like we do.

The team is careful to say this doesn't prove spiders dream, since they didn't measure brain activity. But it suggests that features of sleep we thought belonged to vertebrates may have evolved in very different nervous systems too.

Sleep might be far older and more universal than we imagined.

James Webb discovers a scorching lava planet with a "giant sunscreen"

ScienceDaily

TOI-561 b, about 280 light-years away, circles its star in under 11 hours and should have lost its atmosphere long ago. But after Webb watched it for 37 hours, covering nearly four orbits and seeing its permanent night side for the first time, astronomers found it reflects about 75% of incoming starlight, roughly as much as Venus.

The best explanation is a layer of silicate (rock-vapor) clouds acting as a shield, while a global ocean of molten rock keeps feeding fresh gas into the sky.

A planet that wears clouds made of stone.

Mathematical puzzle from 1904 takes physical form in gold-studded helium droplets

Phys.org

The Thomson problem asks how identical electric charges arrange themselves on a sphere to reach the lowest energy, and it has exact answers only for small numbers. A team at the University of Innsbruck charged superfluid helium droplets, a hair above absolute zero, and sprayed them with gold vapor. Gold clumped exactly where each charge sat, leaving a "fingerprint" of the pattern once the helium evaporated.

The imprints matched the theoretical best arrangements, and bigger droplets showed hexagonal tilings with occasional five-sided defects, the same geometry seen in viruses and soccer-ball molecules.

For once, the experiment solves the puzzle and the math has to catch up.

Five new mussel species reveal how much biodiversity US rivers still conceal

Mongabay

Scientists from Shedd Aquarium, Arkansas State University and the USGS described five new freshwater "hickorynut" mussels from rivers across the southern United States. The shells looked just like known species, so the team compared DNA from three genes and more than 440 shell outlines to tell them apart.

Mussels quietly filter algae, sediment and bacteria out of rivers, which makes them keystone species. Several of the new ones live in a single river basin, so naming them is the first step to protecting them.

Even in well-studied rivers, there are still neighbors nobody had met.

Taiwan's tea growers let nature take back control as climate pressures mount

Mongabay

In the Alishan mountains, famous for high-mountain oolong, a handful of growers have dropped chemical fertilizers and pesticides, planting cover crops and letting pruned branches mulch the soil. The most radical method, yefang ("wild-grown"), means no pruning, no watering, just tea bushes growing under deep-rooted trees.

Sixth-generation farmer Lin Kuang-li says his soil now soaks up and drains water much better. During a harsh spring drought, the wild-grown plants survived while many conventional ones died. Yields are far lower, though, so fewer than ten growers in Alishan farm this way so far.

Sometimes the most resilient farm is the one you leave alone.

USP desenvolve "nave espacial" no celular para ensinar crianças cegas a digitar

Jornal da USP

O app Digitavox USP para Crianças transforma os exercícios de digitação em uma missão espacial: o teclado físico, ligado ao celular por um adaptador, vira o painel de uma nave quebrada, e a criança precisa digitar certo para salvar alienígenas. Aprender a digitar vira brincadeira, e brincadeira vira autonomia.

Compararam a escola com o Parque CienTec, dentro do Parque Estadual das Fontes do Ipiranga.

Ciência de verdade, feita por adolescentes, provando que árvore é filtro.

Technician cracks 1,600-year-old mystery to replicate color-changing effect of Roman glass masterpiece

Phys.org

The Lycurgus Cup, a 4th-century Roman cup in the British Museum, looks jade green when light bounces off it and glows ruby red when light shines through. The trick is believed to come from tiny amounts of gold and silver forming nanoparticles smaller than a wavelength of light. Nanotechnology, about 1,600 years early.

Lisa Hollands, a technician at the University of Sheffield, spent many experiments tuning furnaces, oxygen levels and even adding sugar until the metals settled into the glass the right way. Glass artist Elliot Walker used her recipe for a new sculpture, on show in Sheffield in November.

Roman glassmakers did by craft what we now explain with quantum optics.

The mathematics of growing a shell — recreating diverse mollusk shells with just three inputs

Phys.org

Caltech researchers Kaushik Bhattacharya and Huan Liu showed that almost every known mollusk shell can be generated from just three things: the shape of the growing edge, how fast it scales up, and how much it twists each step. Repeat that one rule and a snail, a clam or a nautilus emerges.

The model holds up even when growth speeds change or small defects appear, and each class of shelled mollusk lives in its own narrow range of growth rates. The authors think this gentle, step-by-step way of building could inspire new manufacturing methods.

Next time you pick up a seashell, you're holding a tiny algorithm.

The Kuji Amber Museum in Kuji, Japan

Atlas Obscura

Kuji, on the coast of Iwate in northern Japan, is the country's best-known amber region, and this museum is devoted entirely to the golden fossil resin. Inside you'll find raw chunks, carved pieces and even an amber chess set.

The best part: when the weather cooperates, visitors can dig for their own amber at the museum's mining area.

A treasure hunt where the treasure is millions of years old.

Love with nowhere to go

Aeon · cerca de 23 minutos

Philosopher and counsellor Derek van Zoonen argues that grief isn't only about death. We also grieve a friendship that faded, a parent changed by dementia, a version of our life that didn't happen. He calls grief "love with nowhere to go."

Because our culture mostly recognises bereavement, these other losses often go unnamed, and people struggle to understand what they feel. A gentle, thoughtful essay about why naming a loss helps us see what we loved.

Slow reading for a quiet moment, not a sad one.
