Chandra Finds Possible Origin of 'Treble Clef' Waves

NASA

Astronomers spotted a structure of radio emission that looks remarkably like a treble clef, complete with a tall vertical stroke and swooping curls, in a region where galaxy clusters are colliding. Combining X-ray data from NASA's Chandra with infrared and radio observations, they think they know how it got there.

The best explanation is a two-step story: first a supermassive black hole blew out a burst of energetic particles, and later the collision of two galaxy clusters stirred and stretched that material into its musical shape.

The universe, apparently, also likes to sign its work with a bit of sheet music.

Quantum sensor could measure how Earth drags spacetime

Phys.org

Einstein predicted that a spinning mass like Earth slightly twists the spacetime around it, an effect called frame-dragging. Until now, measuring it took dedicated satellites. Researchers from UC Berkeley, Caltech and the Austrian Academy of Sciences propose doing it in a lab instead.

Their design is a doughnut-shaped tube of superfluid helium, cooled to below −271 °C, where rotation shows up as a tiny quantum phase difference across a Josephson junction. If the needed ultra-cold components work, the tabletop device could match space-based sensitivity in less time and for far less money.

Feeling the planet's spin warp space, from inside a basement lab.

Mysterious brain activity under anesthesia challenges what we know about consciousness

ScienceDaily

Working with epilepsy surgery patients, a team at Baylor College of Medicine recorded individual neurons in the hippocampus while the patients were under general anesthesia and heard speech. Even with no awareness at all, the neurons told nouns from verbs and adjectives, and even anticipated which word would come next in a sentence.

The neurons also got better over time at noticing unexpected sounds, so some learning keeps going in the "switched off" brain. The findings blur the line between consciousness and processing, and could help future speech prostheses.

Part of you keeps listening to the story even when "you" aren't there.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Drowned 5,000-year-old forests reborn as thriving peat reefs off Mozambique

Phys.org

In the shallow waters of Mozambique's Bazaruto Archipelago, researchers found ancient forests, between 300 and 5,000 years old, that the sea swallowed long ago. Their remains became peat platforms on the seabed, and these turned out to be full of life.

Each "peat reef" hosts 26 to 36 species of fish, plus lobsters, oysters and young snappers, a richness comparable to nearby coral reefs even without any living coral. The peat also holds up to 40% organic carbon, and the team suspects similar habitats are waiting to be found along other tropical coasts.

An entire ecosystem nobody had on the map, built on a forest's second life.

Ascension Island: How a planting project created a lush cloud forest

Phys.org

When Charles Darwin passed through Ascension, a volcanic speck in the middle of the Atlantic, it was nearly bare, with only a handful of dwarf ferns. His friend, the botanist Joseph Hooker, planned a way to "harvest" moisture from the clouds, and over five decades seeds from botanical gardens around the world were planted on the island.

Today the summit is crowned by a humid tropical forest just meters away from desert slopes. Scientists are studying this rare, well-documented case of an ecosystem built from scratch for lessons on how to help damaged ones recover.

Almost everywhere, forests become deserts. Here it went the other way.

Mangroves gain ground globally, marking turnaround after decades of loss

Mongabay

Satellite mapping by Global Mangrove Watch across 108 countries shows that, after decades of steady loss, the world's mangroves ended 1985–2025 with a small net gain of 477 km². It's modest, about 0.3%, but it is a gain.

India led the way with about 1,180 km² of new mangroves, helped by community restoration and clever "fish-bone" canals that bring tidal water to the right spots. Indonesia still had the biggest losses, so the work is far from done.

A rare environmental chart where the line finally bends upward.

The Library Is Closed. Feel Free to Come In.

Reasons to be Cheerful

Small-town libraries in Minnesota, Washington, Utah, California and Kansas are trying something simple: register your library card, and you can swipe in and use the building even when no staff are on duty. People use it to work, study, apply for jobs, use a computer, or just get out of bad weather.

Timberland Regional Library in Washington logged more than 63,000 swipes across 12 branches in three years. Cameras, a short safety briefing and quick revocation of access have kept things calm, though librarians stress it complements, rather than replaces, staffed hours.

A public space that trusts its public, and it works.

ESA's New Sentinel 3C Satellite Makes its Debut with Sumptuous Images of Italy and the Arctic

Good News Network

Only two weeks after launch, Europe's new Copernicus Sentinel-3C sent home its first pictures: a crisp natural-color Italy, the icy channels of the Canadian Arctic and the turquoise Caribbean.

The satellite measures ocean color and temperature, sea level, sea-ice thickness and the height of Greenland's ice sheet. That data helps track droughts, wildfires, marine pollution and the health of vegetation, and it's freely available to scientists.

Thorla-McKee Well in Caldwell, Ohio

Atlas Obscura

In 1814, two men drilling in Ohio were after salt, pumping up brine to boil down. What kept coming up with it was a black, sticky nuisance: petroleum.

With no idea what to do with it, they eventually bottled it and sold it as "Seneca Oil," a supposed medicine. The little well survives as a reminder of how one of the world's most important resources began as somebody's annoying byproduct.

History's biggest industries sometimes start as "ugh, what's this gunk?"

Steaming Tender Restaurant in Palmer, Massachusetts

Atlas Obscura

Palmer Union Station opened in 1884 and was once one of the busiest stations in the United States, where seven competing rail lines met. It closed in 1971 and sat abandoned for years.

Then Blake and Robin Lamothe bought it and spent 17 years restoring it, piece by piece. Today the old station is a restaurant, and you can have dinner inside a piece of railroad history.

Seventeen years of patience, served as dinner.

O triunfo da viola no campo e na cidade

Revista Pesquisa FAPESP

O violeiro e sociólogo Luiz Antonio Guerra, da UFG, transformou sua tese premiada no livro Uma sociologia da viola caipira (Edusp), com entrevistas com mais de 60 violeiros. A viola chegou com os jesuítas no século 16, perdeu espaço nas cidades para o violão e virou símbolo do interior de São Paulo, Minas e Goiás.

Com o êxodo rural do século 20, ela voltou para a cidade na bagagem dos migrantes e hoje está em palcos, universidades e em mais de 250 orquestras de viola só no estado de São Paulo.

Dez cordas que contam a história do Brasil saindo do campo e indo para a cidade.

Welcome to the 'Uncanny Valley,' the Haas Brothers' Fantastic and Cheeky Universe

Colossal

Twins Nikolai and Simon Haas have spent more than 15 years building a world of furry creature-stools, glowing strawberry trees and plush beings that seem to have wandered out of a friendly alien cartoon. Their mid-career retrospective is now at the Blanton Museum in Austin, the city where they grew up.

The show plays with the "uncanny valley": the idea that adding a touch of cuteness to strange, almost-human objects makes us feel tenderness instead of discomfort.

Ann Toebbe's Skewed Paintings Transform Architectural Spaces into Cherished Places

Colossal

Chicago painter Ann Toebbe draws houses and rooms as if they'd been unfolded on a table: walls opened out, floors tilted up, indoors and outdoors in the same frame. She mixes gouache, oil, paper collage and colored pencil, with obsessive attention to wallpaper, rugs and fabric patterns.

The distortion is deliberate. Like a memory, she stretches a wall here and adds a garden there "to make a layout snap together." Her work is on view at Andrew Rafacz gallery in Chicago through October 31.

Floor plans that feel the way home feels, not the way it measures.

📜 Neste dia

15 de outubro de 1582: o dia que veio depois do dia 4

Wikipédia

Há exatos 444 anos, Itália, Espanha, Portugal e Polônia dormiram numa quinta-feira, 4 de outubro, e acordaram na sexta-feira, 15. Dez dias simplesmente sumiram do calendário: era a estreia do calendário gregoriano, criado para corrigir o atraso acumulado pelo antigo calendário juliano em relação às estações. É o calendário que usamos até hoje, inclusive para saber que hoje é dia 15.

The ladder of knowledge

Aeon · Alexander V Gheorghiu

About a century ago, David Hilbert believed every mathematical problem could be solved. Then Kurt Gödel proved that any system of axioms leaves true statements it can't prove. That's usually told as a story about the limits of knowledge.

UCL logician Alexander Gheorghiu offers a gentler reading. Truth isn't a ceiling we keep failing to reach. It's more like a ladder we build as we go, and every new rung opens room for the next. About 3,100 words, with Wittgenstein, Russell and Dummett along the way. Good company for a slow second coffee.
