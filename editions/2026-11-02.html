A dust-hidden star cluster may be nearly as old as the universe

Phys.org

VVV CL001 is a globular cluster tucked behind thick dust near the center of the Milky Way, about 24,000 light-years away. Astronomers led by INAF’s Capodimonte Observatory combined infrared images from a 6.5-meter Magellan telescope with Hubble data, used machine learning to pick out ~155 member stars, and dated it to about 13.4 billion years — just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. It may be the oldest cluster in our galaxy; a spectroscopic follow-up is planned to check its chemistry.

Why it’s cool: some of the stars above us are almost as old as time itself.

30,000-year-old DNA shows Ice Age Europeans were far more connected than we thought

ScienceDaily

A University of Vienna-led team studied the Gravettians, who lived across Europe 34,000–25,000 years ago, combining rare skeletons with human DNA pulled straight from cave sediment — even from a cave with no bones at all. A woman from Isturitz Cave, in southwestern France, carried ancestry from both western and central-eastern Europe, evidence of long-distance mixing. Northern Spain appears to have been a refuge where western lineages survived the coldest part of the Ice Age.

Why it’s cool: we can now read human history from cave dirt.

Scientists shrink Archimedes’ screw to the size of a single molecule

ScienceDaily

Researchers from Augsburg, Copenhagen, the Weizmann Institute and others held a spiral-shaped molecule between two gold tips sharpened down to single atoms. Above a certain voltage, the flowing electrons make the molecule spin — and flipping the polarity reverses it, like a 2,000-year-old water screw run backwards. While it rotates, the molecule conducts electricity up to 100 times better, so the motion itself controls the current.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

An Azores island turns off its lights so baby seabirds can find the sea

Good News Network

Young Cory’s shearwaters leave their nests at night and navigate toward the ocean — but town lights confuse them and many crash-land on the streets. So for the last five nights of October, tiny Corvo (400-some residents) switches off its public lighting from 9pm to 4am. Together with volunteers from the Portuguese bird society SPEA, the Azores have rescued more than 30,000 fledglings since 2015, about 6,000 of them on Corvo alone.

Why it’s cool: a whole island agreeing to sit in the dark for some birds.

Two new bandicoot species, discovered in museum drawers

Phys.org · The Conversation

Over ten years, Australian researchers combed through hundreds of bandicoot specimens in museums worldwide, combining DNA with careful skull measurements. They described the little brown bandicoot of South Australia — likely gone for a century — and elevated the New Guinea brown bandicoot to a full species. They also found the quenda once ranged as far as Adelaide and Kangaroo Island.

Why it’s cool: new species are still hiding in old cabinets.

How tiny bats act as architects of the Amazon forest

Mongabay

Some tiny Amazonian bats bite and fold big leaves into tents, hanging out in the understory. Their droppings are full of seeds from wild fruits, which local and Indigenous communities later grow for food. Researcher Bernal Rodríguez Herrera and his team are studying how these little gardeners could help forests regrow and feed people.

🛠️ Tecnologia, História & Curiosidades

A 2,700-year-old mummy head yields the first molecular evidence of aloe in Egyptian embalming

Phys.org

A Marburg University team of chemists and Egyptologists analyzed 24 objects, from mummy remains to old apothecary powders, and found aloe-linked compounds in a skull from the 8th century BC and in an 18th-century “mummy medicine.” They also re-read ancient Egyptian texts for a word that might mean aloe. Only two samples had the traces, so it’s a clue rather than proof — but it suggests medieval tales about aloe in mummies had older roots.

The Giant Squid Museum, Luarca, Spain

Atlas Obscura

In the Asturian fishing town of Luarca, a small museum displays six giant squid alongside other creatures from the deep. A 2014 sea storm wrecked its original harborside building and kept it closed for eight years, until it reopened in a new home. It’s one of the few places on Earth to stand face to face with the animal behind the kraken legends.

Why it’s cool: sea monsters, but real, and in a quiet seaside town.

A small-town church with a complete set of Tiffany windows

Atlas Obscura

St. John’s Episcopal Church in Franklin, Pennsylvania, is one of only seven churches anywhere whose windows all came from Louis Comfort Tiffany’s studios. The stained glass glows with the opalescent colors Tiffany made famous. Visitors can drop in Tuesday to Friday, or book a guided tour.

The self-making world

Aeon · Jessica Riskin

Lamarck — the man who coined the word “biology” — is remembered mostly as the punchline about giraffes stretching their necks. Stanford historian Jessica Riskin argues his real idea was bigger: living things actively shape themselves and their surroundings through what they do. She traces how that view was pushed aside, and how epigenetics, lizard studies, niche construction and the hunting traditions of humpback whales are bringing it back.

Para ler com calma (~4.600 palavras, ou 29 minutos em áudio): uma visão mais viva da evolução.
