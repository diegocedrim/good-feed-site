Glaciares ocultos em crateras de Marte terminam em paredões de até 200 metros

Superinteressante

Na superfície de Marte, gelo exposto vira gás rapidinho. Mas, escondido sob poeira e rochas, ele pode durar milhões de anos — e um estudo identificou 29 paredões abruptos dentro de crateras perto do vulcão Alba Mons, com até 209 metros de altura.

Não são rochas: seriam as frentes de glaciares cobertos de detritos, línguas de gelo que descem até 3 km pela parede da cratera e terminam de repente, como se cortadas a faca.

Por que é legal: ajuda a contar a história do clima marciano — e marca no mapa onde futuros astronautas podem achar água.

This 6-million-year-old whale may have had the most crooked face in mammal history

ScienceDaily

In Peru's Pisco Formation, a desert famous for beautifully preserved marine fossils, researchers described Scaphokogia huachafo, a small extinct relative of sperm whales, about 2.7 m long. Its skull is the most lopsided known in any whale or dolphin — both bony nostrils sit on the left side.

The team first suspected the fossil had been squashed after burial, but the bone shows no fractures: the twist was real. Its downturned, dense snout and flexible neck suggest it hunted along shallow seafloors, a lifestyle no living whale has.

Why it's cool: evolution sometimes tries a wonderfully weird design, and the rocks remember it.

Potatoes grown in simulated lunar soil match Earth-grown performance

Phys.org

Oregon State University researchers grew potatoes through their entire life cycle in five imitation lunar soils. Plain "Moon dirt" stunted them badly, but mixing in 30% worm compost made the plants grow as well as those in rich Oregon farmland.

The organic matter seems to lock up the heavy metals found in lunar regolith, and the tubers kept their nutrition without extra natural toxins. Next up: testing ten varieties to find the best Moon potato.

Why it's cool: the first lunar farmers may owe a lot to humble earthworms.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

El Niño Rains Paint the Atacama

NASA Earth Observatory

An unusually wet winter in northern Chile woke up seeds that had been waiting in the sand, and the driest desert on Earth burst into flower. Landsat images from September and October show brown ground near Copiapó turning to patches of green and red-purple.

The purple is likely pata de guanaco, and white hillsides are probably suspiros. Chilean botanists say this may be the most extensive "desierto florido" since satellites started watching.

Why it's cool: a bloom so big you can see it from space.

Red crabs begin annual march to sea on Australia's Christmas Island

Phys.org

With the first rains, more than 100 million red crabs have left the forests of Christmas Island, a speck in the Indian Ocean, to walk to the coast and breed. They time their spawning to the pre-dawn receding high tide during the moon's last quarter.

The population had fallen to around 50 million when invasive yellow crazy ants took over their burrows; ant control has helped it bounce back. The crabs are the island's keystone species, keeping the forest healthy.

World Anteater Day: Rewilding and restoration offer hope for enigmatic species

Mongabay

Today is World Anteater Day. In 2025 a giant anteater showed up in Argentina's Alma Guaraní reserve, in the Gran Chaco, for the first time since 1986 — and spent most of its time in areas left to recover on their own after fences kept out cattle, dogs and hunters.

On the Brazilian side, a camera in Rio Grande do Sul's Espinilho State Park filmed one in 2023, the first in that region since 1890. It probably walked over from Iberá, where about 110 anteaters have been released.

Why it's cool: give the land a break and the tamanduá-bandeira finds its way home.

Siblings Build 430-Step 'Sky Ladder' for Their Mother Who Refuses to Live in the City

Good News Network

After her husband died, an 86-year-old woman in China's Zhejiang Province moved to town, but she missed her mountain — and the old path up had vanished under brush. Her daughter Huang Zhulian, 58, decided to fix that.

From August to October the family carried stones, mixed cement and cooked for each other, with a grandson using a heavy-lift drone to fly materials up. Some steps are bolted to a sheer cliff; the mother chipped in from her savings, and now hikers and herb gatherers use the path too.

Why it's cool: love, measured in 430 steps.

When a Dog Bit a Group of Young Boys in 1885, It Sent Them on a Trans-Atlantic Adventure That Changed Medicine

Smithsonian Magazine

In late 1885, a group of working-class boys in New Jersey were bitten by a dog feared to be rabid — at the time, a death sentence. Louis Pasteur had only just tried his new rabies treatment in Paris, so the public chipped in to send the boys across the Atlantic to him.

They came home healthy and briefly famous, and their story helped convince Americans that laboratory science could actually save lives.

Why it's cool: a kids' adventure story that is also the birth of modern medical hope.

The Waterless, Hyper-Efficient Machines That Clean Clothes With Carbon Dioxide

Reasons to Be Cheerful

Squeeze carbon dioxide hard enough and it becomes a liquid that can wash fabric — no water, no detergent, no dryer. Release the pressure and the CO2 turns back into gas, leaving clothes already dry. A Colorado company has spent years refining the idea for outdoor gear and firefighter suits, removing over 95% of toxic soot compounds.

The catch: the energy savings are modest, and shrinking high-pressure tanks for home use is pricey, though LG and Samsung are filing patents.

Why it's cool: laundry physics that sounds like science fiction, already working in the real world.

A hora e a vez do vinho brasileiro

Superinteressante

A reportagem começa num salão em Bento Gonçalves (RS), com 800 pessoas provando 16 vinhos ao mesmo tempo na Avaliação Nacional de Vinhos, escolhidos às cegas entre 558 amostras. Entre os selecionados, 14 gaúchos, um catarinense… e um do Distrito Federal.

O vinho do Cerrado resume o bom momento: novas regiões, técnicas e produtores estão mudando a cara — e o sabor — do vinho feito no Brasil.

Por que é legal: dá vontade de trocar o café por uma taça (mais tarde, claro).

🎨 Arte & Design

Vhils and Marcelo Cidade Cloak a São Paulo Colonial Building in Silver Thermal Blankets

Colossal

At Rua Cardeal Arcoverde, 2878, in Pinheiros, Portuguese artist Vhils and Brazilian artist Marcelo Cidade wrapped an old colonial-style house in the shiny emergency blankets used in rescues, glued on with cornstarch so the arched windows and reliefs still show.

The facade now mirrors passersby and shimmers with the light. The point, they say, is that a building can start to disappear long before it's demolished — and wrapping it makes you actually look.

Why it's cool: a gleaming silver house on a São Paulo street you may have walked past.

How M.C. Escher's Art Creates Bizarre Mathematical Worlds

Quanta Magazine

Escher said he "never got a pass mark" in math. Yet after seeing the Alhambra's tiles in 1922, he taught himself the symmetries of tilings so well that a crystallographer later said he understood color symmetry better than the experts.

A friendship with geometer H.S.M. Coxeter led to his hyperbolic "Circle Limit" prints, and his work later inspired Roger Penrose and a 2003 mathematical decoding of "Print Gallery." A new exhibition at MoMath in New York runs until February 2027.

Hand-Marbled Papers Ground Sylvain Lefebvre's Surreal Landscapes

Colossal

French artist Sylvain Lefebvre collages hand-marbled papers under layers of oil, acrylic and ink, so the swirls become bark, mist and river. Giant trees tower over tiny people and animals in a forest where everyone seems to belong.

"Journeys Into the Unseen Forest" is at Rebecca Hossack Art Gallery in London through December 23.

Linguagem corporal: o que ela realmente diz?

Superinteressante

Tudo começa com Hans Esperto, o cavalo alemão do início do século 20 que "resolvia" contas batendo a pata. Um psicólogo descobriu o truque sem truque: Hans lia sinais minúsculos e involuntários no corpo de quem perguntava — acertava 89% quando via a pessoa, e só 6% quando apenas ouvia.

A partir daí, a reportagem de capa da Super separa o que a ciência sabe de verdade sobre gestos, posturas e expressões do monte de mitos de "como saber se alguém está mentindo". Ótima para ler com calma.
