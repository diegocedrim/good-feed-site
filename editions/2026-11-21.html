A cryptography trick finds a pulsar hiding in 15 years of data

NASA Science

Some pulsars are almost impossible to spot because their faint gamma-ray ticks are buried across years of observations. Researchers borrowed a “lattice” method from cryptography, devised at Israel's Weizmann Institute, and pointed it at 15 years of public data from NASA's Fermi telescope. It found PSR J1622−7204, a star spinning 261 times per second — using about 10 hours on a single processor, where the best previous method failed even with the equivalent of 600 processor-years.

Por que é legal: uma ideia feita para quebrar códigos acabou servindo para ouvir o batimento de uma estrela morta.

After 8 years in space, an X-ray telescope bound for Mercury opens its eyes

ScienceDaily

An X-ray telescope riding toward Mercury has finally made its first observations of the cosmos, after eight years and some 10 billion kilometres of travel — and it is performing better than its team expected. Its real job starts at the destination: mapping the chemistry of Mercury's surface to help explain how the smallest planet in the Solar System came together.

Por que é legal: oito anos de paciência, e o instrumento acordou melhor do que se esperava.

A fish as old as Montana: the blue sucker lives well over 100 years

Phys.org

Scientists thought the blue sucker, a humble river fish of the Upper Missouri, lived about 40 years. By dating its ear stones (otoliths) with the carbon signature left by mid-century bomb tests, they found individuals up to 130 years old — meaning some fish swimming today hatched in the late 1800s, around when Montana became a state.

Por que é legal: tem peixe no rio que já nadava lá antes da invenção do avião.

The British island that made a deal with nature

Mongabay · vídeo

Lundy, a small island in the Bristol Channel, spent decades on patient conservation — and thousands of seabirds came back, along with richer marine life around its shores. Local fishermen helped design the protected waters, including the UK's first no-take zone. Today the island supports wildlife, a working fishing community and thousands of visitors a year, all at once.

Quechua community in Peru builds an Andean bear camera-trap project on its own reserve

Mongabay

A Quechua community in the Peruvian Andes runs its own camera-trap project to get to know the spectacled bears that share its land. Volunteer ranger Maribel Pacco describes watching a mother and her cubs feeding on bromeliads, and the cameras have already identified ten individual bears. It's science done by the people who live alongside the animals.

Peste, sujeira e mau cheiro: como era a higiene no Palácio de Versalhes

Superinteressante

Por trás do ouro e dos espelhos, a corte de Luís XIV tinha medo de água: a medicina da época acreditava que o banho quente abria os poros para doenças, e a sujeira serviria de proteção. A Roman cup in a 2,000-year-old tomb in Mongolia

Smithsonian Magazine

Archaeologists in Mongolia opened a 2,000-year-old grave of the Xiongnu, pastoral horse-riders who dominated the steppes of Central Asia for about three centuries. Among hundreds of objects was a Roman cup, alongside goods linked to Han China and the Korean peninsula. Together they sketch a trade network stretching across the ancient world long before anyone called it the Silk Road.

Rembrandt's “Night Watch” is teeming with life

Smithsonian Magazine

Researchers swabbed the surface of Rembrandt's 17th-century masterpiece and found a whole hidden community of microorganisms living on it. One of the most puzzling residents is a bacterium first described in 2013 — at a sugar refinery in Japan. Understanding who lives on the canvas helps conservators protect it for the next few centuries.

Por que é legal: a pintura mais famosa da Holanda é, literalmente, um ecossistema.

21 de novembro de 1676: Ole Rømer mostra que a luz tem velocidade

Wikipédia

Observando os eclipses de Io, lua de Júpiter, o astrônomo dinamarquês Ole Rømer notou que eles “atrasavam” quando a Terra estava mais longe do planeta. Diante da Academia de Ciências de Paris, concluiu que a luz não é instantânea: ela leva tempo para viajar. Foi a primeira estimativa da velocidade da luz — a mesma luz que hoje leva um dia inteiro para alcançar a Voyager 1.

The horse made the state

Aeon · ensaio de Lhamsuren Munkh-Erdene

The textbook story says civilisation grew out of wheat fields and granaries. This essay, by a Mongolian scholar, makes a bold case for a different origin: mobility. Horse-riding pastoralists of the steppe, it argues, and the long-distance movement they mastered, were what first knit scattered peoples into large organised societies. A deep-history read that pairs nicely with the Xiongnu tomb above.
