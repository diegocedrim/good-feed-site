Cosmic House of Mirrors

NASA

The James Webb Space Telescope pointed at the galaxy cluster MACS J0454.1-0300, whose enormous mass bends the space around it. Light from galaxies sitting far behind the cluster gets magnified, smeared into arcs and even copied several times over, like reflections in a funhouse.

Astronomers use this natural "gravitational lens" as a free extra telescope, peeking at galaxies that would otherwise be too faint to see.

Some of those smudges are the same galaxy, seen more than once.

Tiny marine microbe sheds new light on how little genetic machinery a cell needs to survive

Phys.org

Teams from the University of Nottingham and Japan's University of Tsukuba found an archaeon in ocean plankton with just 189 protein-coding genes, less than half of any archaeal genome known before. They named it Candidatus Sukunaarchaeum mirabile, after a tiny god in Japanese mythology.

It kept the tools to copy its DNA and build proteins but threw away almost everything for making its own food and energy, so it probably lives off another organism. It sits right on the blurry line between a cell and something more like a virus.

A near-minimum recipe for life, found floating in seawater.

A faint radio whisper from 9 billion years ago could help unlock dark energy

ScienceDaily

Canada's CHIME radio telescope picked up the very faint glow of hydrogen gas from when the universe was about five billion years old. Pulling it out of the noise took 94 nights of data from 2019 and more than a year of checking.

The result shows that mapping hydrogen over huge volumes of space really works, giving astronomers a faster, cheaper way to measure how the universe's expansion has sped up. There are nearly seven more years of data still waiting to be analysed.

An Instafamous giant water lily is confirmed as a new species — and critically endangered

Mongabay

Botanist Carlos Magdalena spotted photos of unusual giant lilies on a nursery owner's Instagram. Satellite images and a five-day expedition with Indigenous guides on Colombia's Inírida River confirmed a new species: Victoria colombiana, a cousin of our Amazonian vitória-régia, with leaves up to 2 metres wide.

Only 30 to 50 plants are thought to exist in the wild, so seeds are now being grown in botanic gardens and near local Indigenous communities.

A plant big enough to see from space was hiding in plain sight on social media.

These South American Birds Can Match a Jackhammer's Ear-Splitting Volume

Smithsonian Magazine

Two birds Brazilians know well, the araponga (bare-throated bellbird) and the seriema (red-legged seriema), turn out to be among the loudest birds ever measured. Their calls go past 120 decibels, about as loud as a chainsaw or a jackhammer.

That puts them in the top tier of animal shouters, and means a forest "ferreiro" really is as loud as it sounds.

Next time an araponga rings in the woods, you'll know it's world-class.

Nearly 200-year-old Tortoise Becomes First to Contribute Extraordinary Epigenome to Aging Research

Good News Network

Jonathan, an Aldabra giant tortoise on the island of St Helena, hatched around 1832 and is thought to be the oldest living land animal. A study in Science Advances is the first to map a giant tortoise's epigenome, the switches that turn genes on and off.

Researchers found 287 gene variants tied to DNA repair, inflammation and cancer protection, and saw that Jonathan's key switches still look a lot like a young tortoise's.

He was already a grown-up when Dom Pedro II was a boy.

Alunas de escola pública de Sergipe ganham prêmios de ciência no Paraguai

Só Notícia Boa

Única escola pública brasileira na ExpoAurora, em Luque, o Colégio Estadual José Inácio de Farias, de Monte Alegre de Sergipe, levou dois primeiros lugares entre 450 projetos de nove países.

Gen Z Is Rescuing Millions in Medical Supplies

Reasons to be Cheerful

Hospitals throw away huge amounts of sealed, perfectly good supplies, like masks, syringes and sutures, as expiry dates get close. Ben Kurian, a 16-year-old in Ohio, started Project MedBridge to catch that surplus, and his first shipment was about 30,000 pounds of gear worth around $250,000.

He's part of a wider wave of student groups that sort, check and send this material to under-resourced clinics in the US and in more than 14 countries.

World's Largest 'Glacial Archeology' Expedition Recovers 48 Stone Age Arrowheads and Shafts

Good News Network

In Norway's Jotunheimen mountains, the "Secrets of the Ice" team recovered 48 arrows and arrowheads made of slate, bone and even freshwater mussel shell, between 2,000 and 6,000 years old. Some still have their wooden shafts and look almost new.

Ice with no oxygen keeps out the bacteria that normally rot wood and bone. Since 2006 the project has logged more than 5,000 artifacts, including the oldest ski known anywhere.

An arrow lost by a reindeer hunter, kept fresh in nature's freezer for millennia.

A Community-Led Archaeology Dig on a Tiny Scottish Isle Has Unearthed a Medieval Monastery of 'International Importance'

Smithsonian Magazine

Local volunteers digging on a tiny Scottish island have uncovered a settlement founded by an early Christian saint in the 500s. It's turning out to be far more than a small island church: it was a real monastery, and probably much more important than anyone had realised.

The finds suggest the site played a bigger role in spreading early Christianity across Britain than historians had credited.

Rios enterrados: a história dos rios que São Paulo escondeu

Jornal da USP

Uma tese de doutorado de Wellington Tibério conta como São Paulo canalizou e cobriu seus rios, do Tamanduateí, retificado já em 1848, às avenidas de fundo de vale que se multiplicaram a partir dos anos 1970. Muita gente hoje mora em cima de um rio sem saber.

Para o pesquisador, o apagamento foi duplo: some o rio da paisagem e some também da memória. Coletivos de arte e projetos de bairro agora tentam trazer esses cursos d'água de volta, com placas, mapas e intervenções.

Symmetry Meets the Sacred in Shuling Guo's Dreamy, Diffused Paintings

Colossal

Shuling Guo grew up in Chaoshan, in southern China, visiting temples full of Daoist, Buddhist and folk imagery. Her oil and coloured-pencil paintings are soft, symmetrical and glowing, with flowers and birds that look like they're seen through mist, somewhere between Early Renaissance altarpieces and Georgia O'Keeffe.

Her show Temple II, made during the year her daughter was born and her grandmother died, runs at Hollis Taggart in New York from 15 October to 5 December and includes her first mural.

Oculus Tower

Atlas Obscura

Near Ferrara, in Italy, a seven-storey ring of concrete was once the heart of a distillery that turned sugar-beet molasses into alcohol. Its centre is completely open to the sky, so light pours down through tier after tier of empty arches.

Factory work is long gone, and what's left feels like a modern-day Colosseum, industrial architecture that ended up beautiful almost by accident.

How Does Life Unfold? A Landscape Metaphor Comes Into Its Own

Quanta Magazine · Philip Ball

In the 1950s, biologist Conrad Waddington pictured a developing cell as a ball rolling down a hilly landscape, choosing valleys that lead it to become skin, nerve or muscle, with genes like hidden ropes tugging the terrain into shape. For decades it was just a pretty metaphor.

Philip Ball shows how single-cell sequencing now lets scientists actually draw these landscapes from real data, and how they turn out to shift and reshape as the cell travels. That could help with regenerating tissue and steering cancer cells, too. A good, unhurried read for the weekend.
