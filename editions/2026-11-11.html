🔬 Ciência & Espaço

Gás fora de época indica que os vulcões de Marte estão só adormecidos

Superinteressante

A sonda ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter, da ESA, detectou picos de cloreto de hidrogênio logo acima de fissuras do vulcão gigante Alba Mons. Na Terra, esse gás escapa quando o magma sobe e se descomprime — então Marte talvez não seja um planeta tão "morto" quanto parece. Ainda não se viu lava nova, mas a pista se soma aos tremores marcianos já registrados.

Regiões vulcânicas ativas podem ser os melhores lugares para procurar sinais de vida.

Chuva de meteoros com bolas de fogo poderá ser vista no Brasil hoje

Só Notícia Boa

As Táuridas do Norte chegam ao pico na madrugada de 11 para 12 de novembro e são famosas por produzir, de vez em quando, bolas de fogo bem brilhantes. Com lua nova, o céu fica mais escuro e a observação melhora. Não precisa de telescópio: basta um lugar longe das luzes da cidade e um pouco de paciência.

Um programa perfeito para hoje à noite — sem tela nenhuma.

Bennu asteroid analysis suggests unexpected origins for organics in space

Phys.org

Amino-acid-like building blocks of proteins show up both in samples brought back from asteroid Bennu and in the famous Murchison meteorite that fell in Australia in 1969. The two rocks came from very different corners of the solar system, yet new research suggests their molecules were made by the same chemical process. That hints the recipe for life's ingredients may be common and robust out there.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

A plan to save Bali's vanishing fireflies takes root in organic farming

Mongabay

On a family farm in Bali, the project Bring Back the Light breeds fireflies and has already released nearly 20,000 of them. Its founder argues that releases alone aren't enough: the insects need pesticide-free fields and healthy wetlands to stay. So the work is as much about organic farming as it is about the fireflies themselves.

Rice paddies glowing again at night — a lovely measure of a healthy landscape.

World's Driest Desert Erupts into Record-Breaking Bloom Boom

Good News Network

Chile's Atacama, the driest non-polar desert on Earth, is covered in flowers again, with around 200 species blooming at once. Some have popped up in spots where nobody had seen them since before the year 2000. Seeds can wait dormant in the sand for years until enough rain arrives.

Patience, encoded in a seed.

Octopuses Are the Ultimate Escape Artists. Now Scientists Know How They Squeeze Through Tiny Holes

Smithsonian Magazine

Everyone knows octopuses can slip through gaps far smaller than their bodies, but nobody had mapped exactly how. A new study breaks the trick down step by step, showing the sequence of moves they use to funnel themselves through a hole. Engineers are paying attention: the same strategy could inspire soft robots for search-and-rescue.

Professora incentiva menino de 9 anos a fazer salto no balé; ele consegue e os dois choram

Só Notícia Boa

Em Caruaru (PE), uma professora de dança ficou ao lado do pequeno Arthur, de 9 anos, até ele conseguir completar um salto difícil no balé — e os dois acabaram chorando de emoção. O vídeo passou de 1 milhão de visualizações. Arthur ainda treina outros três estilos de dança e já é campeão de tango.

Às vezes tudo o que alguém precisa é de uma pessoa dizendo "você consegue".

An 80-Cent Plastic Bag Is Saving Mothers' Lives

Reasons to be Cheerful

A simple plastic pouch called a calibrated drape sits under a mother during childbirth and lets health workers see exactly how much blood she's losing. That early warning has helped cut deaths from postpartum hemorrhage by about two-thirds in African maternity wards. The roundup also features googly-eyed buoys that scare seabirds away from fishing nets.

Proof that some of the best innovations cost less than a coffee.

Paralysis Could Not Stop Her: How This 84-Year-Old Made Good on Her Musical Promise

Good News Network

Bonnie Fay was paralyzed by Guillain-Barré syndrome and spent months recovering in a Pennsylvania care home. While there, she promised to come back one day and play piano for the patients. Now she has kept her word, returning to the same halls to give back the music that helped carry her through.

🛠️ Tecnologia, História & Curiosidades

Roman-era 'frog feast' near Cambridge was the work of owls, bone analysis suggests

Phys.org

Archaeologists digging a ditch near Cambridge found more than 9,000 tiny bones, almost all of them frogs. Was it a Roman delicacy? A strange ritual? Careful analysis of the bones points to a humbler culprit: owls, which swallow prey whole and cough up the bones in pellets, night after night, for years.

A great reminder that not every ancient mystery needs a human explanation.

Osso esquecido por 17 anos revela ave pré-histórica gigante na caverna dos "hobbits"

Superinteressante

Em 2009, arqueólogos acharam metade de um osso de perna na caverna de Liang Bua, na Indonésia — a mesma onde viveu o Homo floresiensis, o "hobbit". O fóssil, com 120 a 190 mil anos, ficou guardado na gaveta até que uma equipe internacional resolveu reexaminá-lo. Era de uma nova espécie de megapódio, uma ave terrestre de grande porte.

Às vezes as maiores descobertas já estão no museu, só esperando um segundo olhar.

Amy Genser's Elaborate Mulberry Paper Assemblages Undulate Like the Sea

Colossal

Amy Genser rolls, layers and paints mulberry paper into wall reliefs that look like coral reefs, ocean swells and seafloor maps seen from above. Thousands of little coils create texture that shifts as you walk past. Her newest monumental piece, "Stars Below," traces a transatlantic sea voyage.

What do a bean and a flamingo have in common? Finding the unexpected in nature photos

Mongabay

Photographer Andrew Zuckerman went through his archive and paired images that rhyme visually — a sprouting bean beside a curving flamingo, for instance. Shot with focus stacking against clean backgrounds, the pairs reveal shared shapes across very different living things. The result is his book United Nature.

A gentle exercise in seeing connections everywhere.

📖 Leitura longa

The Case for a Bubble at Life's Start

Quanta Magazine

How did life begin? One camp says self-copying RNA came first; another says the first step was simply a container — a tiny fatty bubble that kept chemistry concentrated long enough for interesting things to happen. This piece, part of Quanta's series on life's origins, walks through the "lipid-first" idea, the experiments behind it and why membranes may deserve more credit.

A calm, deep read about the oldest question there is.
