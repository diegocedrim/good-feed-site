Suspected Second-generation Planet Solves NASA Hubble Cold Case

NASA / Hubble

Since 1999, the white dwarf HS 0209+0832 carried about a hundred unexplained lines in its Hubble spectrum. Revisiting the archive, astronomers found that many of them match niobium, an element that almost never shows up in white dwarf atmospheres. So much of it suggests the star is swallowing debris from a planet that formed after the star died, out of the material it threw off.

✨ A 27-year-old puzzle, solved by going back through old data — and stars that get a second round of planets.

Sea Monkeys Show Scientists How To Rewrite a Rule of Turbulence

Quanta Magazine

Physicists long assumed that energy in turbulent fluids flows only one way, from big swirls down to ever-smaller ones. Watching swarms of brine shrimp (the classic “sea monkeys”) churn water, researchers found that one simple factor can make that flow run backwards. It’s a lovely case of tiny animals teaching a lesson about the physics of oceans and atmospheres.

✨ The toy from the back of comic books turns out to be a serious lab instrument.

This mysterious hole on the Moon may open into a giant cave

ScienceDaily

NASA picked a project called GIMLI to find out whether the Marius Hills Pit, a deep hole on the Moon, leads into a huge underground cave or lava tube. The plan combines radar, seismic sensors, gravity measurements and cameras to map whatever empty space lies underneath. Lunar caves could hold clues to the Moon’s volcanic past and someday offer astronauts natural shelter from radiation and temperature swings.

✨ Yes, the mission is named after the dwarf from Lord of the Rings. Fitting for exploring caves.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

A first in Brazil: Black-faced lion tamarins vaccinated

Mongabay

In July, field researchers on Superagui Island, in Paraná, vaccinated three black-faced lion tamarins (mico-leão-caiçara) against yellow fever — the first time this species has ever received a vaccine. Fewer than 400 remain in the wild and there is no captive population as backup, so a single outbreak could be devastating. Yellow fever is one of the gravest threats to Brazilian primates, which makes this small step a big deal.

✨ A Brazilian monkey that exists nowhere else, getting its first shot at a safer future.

Orphaned, Rescued, and Rewilded, Female Gorilla Becomes a Mother

Good News Network

Lulingu was orphaned as a baby and taken by wildlife traffickers, then spent years in rehabilitation before becoming one of the first eastern lowland gorillas ever returned to the wild. In May she gave birth in Virunga National Park, in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Since her release she has adapted to a new habitat, a new diet and a new social group — and now she is a mother.

✨ A conservation story that went all the way from rescue to a new generation.

Probiotics prevented coral bleaching during a Red Sea heat wave

Mongabay

During a 2022 marine heat wave, one patch of reef off Saudi Arabia held up better than its neighbours. Those corals had been treated with probiotics — beneficial bacteria isolated from healthy corals — and they largely avoided bleaching. Brazilian researcher Raquel Peixoto is among the scientists behind the work, which points to a practical tool for helping reefs get through hotter summers.

✨ Coral reefs, it turns out, can benefit from a good gut-health routine too.

Positive News

Emmylou Vaxby is a schoolgirl, an author and a seasoned “mudlark”: for nearly a decade she has searched the muddy foreshore of the Thames at low tide for fragments of the city’s past. A reporter joins her on a hunt to see what the river gives up — small, overlooked pieces of everyday life from centuries ago.

The Hospital That Silenced the Machines

Reasons to be Cheerful

Hospitals are noisy places, full of monitors beeping all night long. One British hospital tried something different: it turned all that bedside noise into digital data, so the alarms no longer have to blare across the ward. Patients finally get to rest, and staff can focus on what actually needs attention.

✨ A small design change that gave people back something precious: quiet.

Há 65 anos, um carro e um submarino colidiram pela primeira (e única) vez na História

Superinteressante

Em agosto de 1961, em Lysekil, na Suécia, um motorista estacionou seu Volvo numa ladeira perto do porto e esqueceu o freio de mão. O carro desceu sozinho e só não caiu na água porque bateu na proa de um submarino da Marinha sueca que estava atracado ali. Ninguém se feriu, e um editor de jornal local que passava por perto registrou a cena — a foto hoje está no Museu Marítimo Sueco.

✨ Até onde se sabe, é a única colisão documentada entre um carro e um submarino.

100 alunos de escolas públicas ajudam a construir satélites brasileiros lançados ao espaço

Só Notícia Boa

No projeto “Escola em Órbita”, da Agência Espacial Brasileira, 50 estudantes de Sorocaba (SP) e 50 de Fortaleza (CE) ajudaram a montar, programar e definir as missões dos satélites EduSat-2 e EduSat-3. Os pequenos satélites foram lançados em 1º de outubro e vão coletar dados sobre problemas reais das cidades, como alagamentos e falta de conectividade.

✨ Tem satélite em órbita agora mesmo com a mão de adolescente brasileiro.

The Roman City of Claudivium May Have Been Hiding in Austria

Smithsonian Magazine

Claudivium was a Roman town that faded from the written record nearly 2,000 years ago. Now archaeologists have mapped a previously unknown settlement on the banks of the Danube in Austria, using geophysical surveys that “see” buried walls without digging — and it may be the lost city.

✨ A whole town, hidden under fields, found with magnets instead of shovels.

Colossal

How Hot Pink Togas and Floral Perfumes Conveyed Power in the Roman Empire

Smithsonian Magazine

Forget the all-white marble image of Rome. A new exhibition at the Glyptotek in Copenhagen shows how colour, cloth and scent signalled status in the ancient empire — from vivid pink garments to floral perfumes. It’s a look at fashion as a language of influence two thousand years ago.

✨ The Romans were far more colourful (and better smelling) than their statues suggest.

Atlas Obscura

Set into a low wall in Limassol, this mosaic at first looks like a scene of sea life: teal petals spread like anemones and shimmering glass discs that catch the light like fish scales. It was made in 2025 by schoolchildren, and it rewards anyone who stops long enough to look closely.

✨ Public art made by kids, hiding in plain sight on a city street.
