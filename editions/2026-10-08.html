🔬 Ciência & Espaço

Scientists accidentally discover a genetic code that breaks the rules of life

ScienceDaily

Nearly all life reads DNA with the same dictionary, where three "stop" words tell the cell a protein is finished. In a single-celled ciliate fished out of a pond in Oxford's University Parks, researchers at the Earlham Institute and Oxford found two of those stop words rewired to mean two different amino acids: TAA now means lysine, TAG means glutamic acid.

Biologists had assumed those two signals always change together. This little protist, found by accident, shows the genetic code is more flexible than the textbooks say.

One of the deepest rules of biology, quietly bent by something living in a park pond.

Researchers May Have Directly Glimpsed an Exoplanet's Magnetic Field for the First Time

Smithsonian Magazine

Using South Africa's MeerKAT array of 64 radio dishes, astronomers picked up radio bursts from Beta Pictoris b, a young gas giant about 12 times Jupiter's mass, 63 light-years away. The signals point to a magnetic field roughly 2,000 times stronger than Earth's — and to auroras glowing over the planet's poles.

It's the first direct detection of a magnetic field on a planet beyond our solar system, a new way to probe what's going on deep inside distant worlds.

Northern lights, heard by radio, from 63 light-years away.

Scientists found Homo erectus 200,000 years earlier than expected

ScienceDaily

At Drimolen, in South Africa's Cradle of Humankind, a team led by La Trobe University dated a Homo erectus fossil to about 2 million years ago — some 200,000 years older than adult specimens found elsewhere, such as at Dmanisi in Georgia.

The same site shows a cousin lineage, Paranthropus robustus, slowly evolving bigger teeth and stronger jaws as the climate changed. Two very different branches of the human family, living side by side.

This "golden tree" coral was too strange for any known family

ScienceDaily

On seamounts off Costa Rica's Isla del Coco, 360 to 529 metres down, researchers led by Odalisca Breedy (Universidad de Costa Rica) found a brilliant yellow, tree-shaped coral more than a metre tall, surrounded by crowds of brittle stars. Its genes didn't fit anywhere — so it got a brand-new family, Laurinqueidae.

The name is pure Tolkien: Laurinque elenya means "golden tree" and "of the stars" in Elvish Quenya.

A real-life golden tree of Valinor, growing in the dark of the Pacific.

Healthy reef sounds can boost coral and fish recovery efforts

Phys.org

In Kāne'ohe Bay, Hawaii, Scripps scientists set out 37 artificial reef structures and, every night for two weeks, played recordings of a healthy reef — fish calls, crackling snapping shrimp — through an underwater speaker.

Structures nearest the speaker got the most baby corals settling on them, and the "noisy" sites drew four to 14 times more fish larvae than silent ones. Turns out young sea creatures choose their home partly by how lively it sounds.

Restoring a reef by playing it its own song.

African Elephants Often Produce Choruses of Low-Pitched Rumbles. Scientists Are Decoding Them

Smithsonian Magazine

Researchers went through 902 recordings made in Kenya's Amboseli National Park between 1986 and 2022, looking at moments when several elephants rumble at once. They found six kinds of chorus, from mother-to-calf "coo" rumbles to "let's go" rumbles that get the group moving and big reunion greetings.

In some choruses the individual voices grow more alike as they overlap — much like people unconsciously picking up each other's way of speaking in a good conversation.

'A few are afraid of the chicks': Preparing Thailand's rescued leopard cats for life in the wild

Mongabay

Every year, 40 to 65 tiny leopard cat cubs are brought to rescue centres in Thailand, many found orphaned in sugarcane fields. Staff nurse them, wean them off formula, then move them into leafy pens for two months of "wild school": climbing, hunting, and learning to avoid people.

Not every student is a natural — some cubs are scared of their first live chick and learn by watching bolder pen-mates. Then, release day: back to the forest.

Só Notícia Boa

Pesquisadora Mariangela Hungria recebe a Medalha Luiz de Queiroz por contribuição à agricultura

Jornal da USP

Formada na Esalq em 1979, Mariangela Hungria passou a carreira mostrando que bactérias do solo podem fazer o trabalho de adubo químico: rizóbios para o feijão, Azospirillum para milho e trigo, e a combinação dos dois na soja, ajudando as plantas a captar nitrogênio do ar.

Depois do World Food Prize em 2025 — o "Nobel da agricultura" —, ela recebe hoje, 8 de outubro, a medalha da própria escola onde se formou, no Salão Nobre da Esalq, em Piracicaba, com transmissão pelo YouTube.

Ciência brasileira invisível a olho nu que está em quase todo prato de feijão com arroz.

How NYC Students Saved School Lunch

Reasons to be Cheerful

Free lunch doesn't help much if nobody wants to eat it. Through the Youth Food Advocates program, New York City teenagers like 16-year-old Leslie Gomez Rivera survey classmates about what they actually like, what bugs them, and why they skip the cafeteria — then bring the results to the adults in charge.

Thanks partly to that work, new menu items now need 70% student approval, more than 200 cafeterias were redesigned with deli-style stations and better seating, and lunch participation in redesigned high schools rose 35%.

Two Chemists Win Nobel Prize for Unraveling a Mystery of Molecular 'Handedness'

Smithsonian Magazine

Many molecules come in left- and right-handed mirror versions, and life almost always uses just one. Labs, though, tend to produce a 50-50 mix. France's Henri Kagan showed in 1986 that a slightly lopsided catalyst can produce a much more lopsided product; Japan's Kenso Soai found a reaction that amplifies itself until almost only one hand is left.

Their work helps explain how life may have ended up "handed" in the first place — and helps drugmakers produce the right version of a medicine, with fewer side effects.

"The world is handed" — and now chemists can choose which hand.

Psychedelic Paintings by Rafael Silveira Explore a World of 'Visual Synonymies'

Colossal

Brazilian painter Rafael Silveira swaps faces for landscapes, flowers and animals, chasing what he calls "visual synonymies" — surprising echoes between shapes that have nothing to do with each other.

For his show Popsicledelic Trip at KP Projects in Los Angeles, some paintings sit in hand-carved wooden frames shaped like popsicles, a wink at the arched panels of Renaissance altarpieces: half devotion, half something that melts.

A Brazilian artist making Renaissance art taste like summer.

More Than 18,000 Wildlife Species Populate Joel Sartore's Ambitious 'Photo Ark' (So Far!)

Colossal

Since 2006, National Geographic photographer Joel Sartore has been photographing animals in zoos, aquariums and sanctuaries against plain black or white backgrounds — every species treated like a portrait sitter. He's at 18,567 species and aims for more than 25,000.

His theory is simple: "It's the eye contact that moves people." Look a tiny frog in the eye, and you start to care about it.

Wikipédia

He was probably right

Aeon · Massimo Pigliucci · ~22 min

The philosopher Massimo Pigliucci revisits Cicero's idea of probabilia: holding beliefs with confidence that matches the evidence — not certainty, not endless doubt. "Follow easily, affirm rarely."

It's a calm, practical essay on how to act firmly while staying open to being wrong: grade what you believe by how well it's been tested, and decide in advance what would change your mind.

An ancient Roman's recipe for a less noisy head.
