🔬 Ciência & Espaço

NASA's Perseverance rover just broke the all-time off-world driving record

ScienceDaily

Perseverance has now driven 45.22 km across Mars, nudging past the 45.16 km that Opportunity took almost 15 years to cover — and it did it in about five and a half. The secret is its AutoNav self-driving system, which weighs some 600,000 terrain points per drive (the little Sojourner rover of 1997 managed about 20).

It's currently exploring ancient rocks in the Lac de Charmes region, looking for traces of old hydrothermal systems that might once have hosted microbes.

A car with no driver, on another planet, setting distance records.

Physicists trap and tame a single particle of antimatter for months

Phys.org

A Northwestern team held one lone positron — the antimatter twin of the electron — suspended by electric and magnetic fields in a near-perfect vacuum, chilled to about 0.3 degrees above absolute zero, for months. Using finely tuned microwaves they could nudge it between quantum energy levels.

The plan is to compare its magnetism with the electron's at extreme precision. If the two turn out to differ even slightly, it could help explain why the universe is made of matter at all.

Keeping antimatter alive is like holding a snowflake in a furnace — for months.

In a first, astronomers identify a star that might glow green

Smithsonian Magazine

Stars are usually red, yellow, white or blue — green isn't supposed to happen. But by reconstructing the colours of distant white dwarfs from the wavelengths of light they emit, researchers found one that would likely look green to human eyes.

Others in the sample would appear purple or pink, depending on the elements in their atmospheres. It's a reminder that these dense stellar embers are far more colourful than we picture them.

Flores rosas tomam a Caatinga da Bahia depois das primeiras chuvas

Só Notícia Boa

Bastou chover para o chão seco do semiárido baiano se cobrir de rosa. As flores são da cebola-brava (Habranthus itaobinus), uma planta que passa a seca inteira escondida como bulbo debaixo da terra, guardando energia.

Quando a água chega, ela brota rapidinho e floresce — em geral uma vez por ciclo. O espetáculo foi registrado em vídeo pelo criador de conteúdo Ciel do Sertão.

A Caatinga ensinando paciência: esperar a seca toda para florescer em poucos dias.

Mesmo sem olhos, ostras e mexilhões possuem grande diversidade de proteínas que percebem a luz

Agência FAPESP

Pesquisadores do Instituto de Biociências da USP analisaram 17 espécies de bivalves, a maioria coletada em São Sebastião (SP), e descobriram que eles “sentem” a luz pela pele. Espécies sem nenhum olho chegam a produzir 9 tipos de opsinas — as proteínas sensíveis à luz —, e algumas com olhinhos simples têm até 12. A retina humana usa 5.

Essas proteínas parecem ajudar em ritmos biológicos e até na cicatrização, funções que a ciência ainda está desvendando.

Uma ostra pode ter mais “sensores de luz” que você.

Sick corals can heal with a “magic touch” from healthy ones

Smithsonian Magazine

A coral is often a colony of tiny, genetically identical animals — and it turns out that relatives help each other. Researchers found that pale, heat-bleached fragments regained their colour within weeks when placed in physical contact with a thriving clone of themselves.

The finding hints at a natural healing mechanism that reef restoration projects could put to work as oceans warm.

10-year-old dog found after a month lost in Yellowstone

Good News Network

Luna, a 10-year-old Jack Russell terrier, slipped out of a cabin at Yellowstone's Canyon Village on September 10 when housekeeping opened the door. She spent more than a month alone in the wild.

A volunteer from an animal recovery group finally caught her in a live trap baited with fabric carrying her owner's scent. Luna was about 10 pounds lighter but alert and in good spirits — and is back home in California.

A train was delayed by a solar storm. But something didn't add up.

ScienceDaily

A classic tale of space weather says a solar storm scrambled the telegraph that controlled railway signals near Exeter, England, delaying the 10:05 p.m. train by 16 minutes in 1841. The catch: the Exeter–Starcross line didn't exist until 1846.

Cross-checking old newspapers and magnetic records, researchers pinned the real date to October 18, 1848. It's still one of the earliest known cases of the Sun disrupting human technology.

History detective work, with the Sun as the suspect.

A Rhode Island man grew the first pumpkin to surpass 3,000 pounds

Smithsonian Magazine

Ron Wallace's pumpkin tipped the scales at 3,156 pounds — roughly 1,430 kg, about the weight of a small car — making it the largest fruit ever recorded. It broke a record that had stood for just one week: British grower Ian Paton's 2,857-pound giant.

Yes, botanically a pumpkin is a fruit. A 1.4-tonne fruit.

Scientists discover a simple, low-energy way to recycle acrylic glass

Good News Network

Researchers at ETH Zurich found a way to break Plexiglas back down into its original building blocks, recovering up to 98% as pure monomers. It needs just one cheap chemical, no solvents, and a bit over 200°C — about half the heat of earlier methods.

Because it works with existing equipment, recycled acrylic could finally be as good as new and cheap enough to make sense.

Álvaro Urbano honors the lost specimens of Mexico City's Casa Cueva

Colossal

In 1954, architect Juan O'Gorman and artist Helen O'Gorman built Casa Cueva inside a natural lava cave in Mexico City. The house was later largely destroyed, but Helen's illustrated book of Mexican flowering plants survived.

Artist Álvaro Urbano turned those drawings into metal botanical sculptures that seem to float like apparitions. The show, “Helen,” is at Museo Jumex until February 2027.

21 de outubro de 1983: o metro passa a ser medido pela luz

Wikipédia

Nesse dia, a Conferência Geral de Pesos e Medidas redefiniu o metro como a distância que a luz percorre no vácuo em 1/299.792.458 de segundo. Antes disso, a referência já tinha sido uma fração do meridiano terrestre, uma barra de platina guardada em Paris e o comprimento de onda de um átomo de criptônio.

Desde então, a régua do mundo é a própria velocidade da luz.

Smithsonian Magazine

A feature that follows researchers on tough expeditions to the bottom of the Arctic Ocean, where ancient communities of bacteria naturally produce hydrogen. The scientists are collecting and documenting these microbes while the ecosystem is still intact, hoping they hold clues to clean fuel.

Deep sea, deep time, and tiny life forms — a good one for a slow morning.
