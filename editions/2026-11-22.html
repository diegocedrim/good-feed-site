Could a clock tick at two different speeds at once?

ScienceDaily

Relativity says time runs a little differently depending on where a clock sits — today's best atomic clocks can feel the difference across just a few millimetres of height. A team led by Kyushu University now proposes a way to catch something stranger: a single clock living in a quantum superposition of several “times” at once. Their trick for controlling the clock's motion would make trapped-ion clocks 100 to 1,000 times more sensitive to the effect. It's still theory, but the experiment looks within reach.

Por que é legal: um teste de bancada que pode tocar na velha briga entre mecânica quântica e gravidade.

A giant star swallowed its companion — and Webb saw the aftermath

ScienceDaily

In the Andromeda galaxy, a small star spiralled into a huge neighbour some 18,000 times brighter than the Sun, setting off an eruption. The James Webb Space Telescope looked 47 days later and caught the scene in new detail, giving astronomers a rare, close-up look at how two stars actually merge into one.

Curiosity spots drifting twilight clouds on Mars

NASA Science · Photojournal

On Halloween night — sol 5,061 of its mission — the Curiosity rover pointed its navigation cameras at the sky after sunset and filmed wispy clouds drifting over Gale Crater. These twilight clouds appear only briefly each Martian autumn, glowing faintly near the horizon. After more than 14 years on the job, the rover still stops to watch the sky.

Por que é legal: um entardecer nublado a 200 milhões de km de casa.

A whale's breath, caught by a drone, is enough to read its whole genome

Good News Network

Researchers including a team from the University of St Andrews flew a small drone carrying sterile petri dishes through the spouts of humpback whales in British Columbia's Kitimat fjords. From 58 breath samples of 26 whales — many collected by flying straight from shore — they sequenced complete genomes for the first time without darts or biopsies. The method even got every whale's sex right and turned up three previously undescribed genetic variants.

The call of the seamount: two new armoured tardigrades

Phys.org

On the Senghor Seamount off Cape Verde, an underwater mountain whose summit rises to just 100–130 metres below the surface, scientists from Senckenberg am Meer found two new species of marine tardigrade. Of 219 water bears collected, 212 came from the summit and only seven from the deep sea nearby. One new species carries genital spines rarely seen in tardigrades; the other keeps juvenile traits, like fewer claws, into adulthood.

Scientists are engineering a comeback for the American elm

Smithsonian Magazine

The American elm once arched over countless streets, until Dutch elm disease nearly wiped it out in the 20th century. Now researchers are working to bring the tree back to the wild — and, eventually, to people's back gardens. It's a long, patient project measured in tree-lifetimes.

Qual é a origem da expressão “rock and roll”?

Superinteressante

Muito antes de virar gênero musical, “rock and roll” era gíria das comunidades negras americanas no começo do século 20 — e aparecia em canções de blues e R&B, como “Good Rockin' Tonight” (1947). Quem popularizou o termo para batizar a nova batida foi o DJ de rádio Alan Freed nos anos 1950. Na década seguinte, com Dylan e Beatles, a coisa encolheu para só “rock” e se abriu em folk, psicodélico, progressivo e heavy metal.

Por que é legal: um bom assunto para puxar no almoço de domingo.

Who was buried in Westminster Abbey's Roman coffin?

ScienceDaily

The limestone coffin bears the name of Valerius Amandinus, a Roman Londoner of the 300s — but a cross carved on its lid is some 600 years younger. Radiocarbon dating now places the bones inside between 987 and 1052, meaning the coffin was recycled in late Saxon times. The most likely occupant, argues historian Warwick Rodwell, is Abbot Aelfwig, who died around 1020. The skeleton itself had been thought lost until the lid was lifted again in 2016.

The secret student gallery in the Paris Catacombs

Atlas Obscura

Deep in a stretch of the Paris Catacombs, once reached by a hidden staircase from the School of Mines, there's a gallery of murals painted by students. Every year around Sainte-Barbe, patron saint of miners (December 4), the first-year class adds its own painting underground — a tradition that continues today, now with the quarry inspectors' blessing.

22 de novembro de 1869: o Cutty Sark vai para a água

Wikipédia

Em Dumbarton, na Escócia, foi lançado o Cutty Sark, um dos clippers mais rápidos já construídos para trazer chá da China. Quando os navios a vapor tomaram essa rota, ele passou a levar lã da Austrália — e chegou a cobrir 360 milhas náuticas em um único dia. O nome vem da camisola de uma bruxa num poema de Robert Burns. Último clipper do chá que sobreviveu, hoje descansa em doca seca em Greenwich, Londres, como navio-museu.

The search for a top dark matter candidate was losing hope. Then came a potential signal

Smithsonian Magazine

For decades, WIMPs — weakly interacting massive particles — were physicists' favourite guess for what dark matter is made of, and huge detectors were built deep underground to catch one. Years of silence wore that enthusiasm down. This feature follows a field that had started to lose heart, and the possible new signal that has brought back a cautious optimism — along with all the hard questions still ahead. A good Sunday read about patience at the edge of what we know.
