Earth's gravity may have kept the early moon's volcanic heart beating

Phys.org

Rocks brought back by China's Chang'e-5 mission are volcanic and only about 2 billion years old, far younger than a small, fast-cooling Moon should allow. A team led by Miao Chen at Chengdu University of Technology suggests the answer is tidal heating: when the young Moon orbited closer to us on a more stretched-out path, Earth's pull kept flexing its insides like a squeezed rubber ball.

Their 3D model shows the friction concentrating in two symmetric hot zones near the core, producing several times more heat than radioactivity alone some 3.2 billion years ago.

Why it's cool: the dark "seas" you see on the full Moon may partly be Earth's own handiwork.

A dead star's mysterious rings reveal what could happen when our Sun dies

ScienceDaily

Around the white dwarf J1328−1450, astronomers using ESO's Very Large Telescope found not one but two rings of rubble left over from the star's shattered planetary system. The rings have different chemistry, hinting they came from different worlds or from different layers of one destroyed planet.

Something seems to keep the gap between them open, most likely two surviving bodies, one only kilometers wide and another possibly as big as Pluto or our Moon.

Why it's cool: a preview, billions of years ahead, suggesting that not everything in a solar system is lost when its star dies.

400,000-year-old teeth reveal a stunning twist in human evolution

ScienceDaily

DNA rarely survives that long, but proteins in tooth enamel can. Analyzing ancient proteins from Homo erectus teeth found in China, researchers found signs that this species mixed with Denisovans, the mysterious cousins of Neanderthals.

Faint traces of that encounter may even have been passed down, through Denisovans, to people living today in Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

As loggerhead turtles spread west, Mediterranean beach resorts learn to share the sand

Mongabay

Loggerheads used to nest mostly in Greece, Türkiye and Cyprus, but as the sea warms they are digging nests ever farther west, along the coasts of Italy, France and Spain. That puts 20-gram hatchlings on the same sand as Italy's packed beach clubs.

Instead of fighting, many places are adapting: volunteers spot tracks with drones and fence off nests, towns like Ascea have switched to softer seafront lighting, and some restaurants dim their lights at hatching time so the babies head for the sea, not the bar.

Why it's cool: a crowded tourist coast learning, little by little, to make room for a 200-kg ancient mariner.

Amazon mapping shows cacao expansion without deforestation in Brazil

Mongabay

Embrapa combined radar and high-resolution satellite images, plus field checks and printed maps that farmers marked themselves, to map every cacao plot in Pará. Planted area grew about a third between 2022 and 2024, and around 96% of it sits on land that had been cleared before 2020.

There is a caveat: most new plantings are full-sun monocultures, which yield more but are more vulnerable to pests than traditional cacao grown under forest shade.

Why it's cool: the chocolate in your future may come from giving old pastures a second life.

Student finds proof of rare wild cat in northern New Mexico

Phys.org

William Grooms, a PhD student at New Mexico State University, set up motion cameras at 120 spots in Carson National Forest during his first field season. Going through the images months later, he found a Canada lynx, the snow-loving cat with tufted ears and huge furry paws.

Experts confirmed it: it's the first documented lynx in New Mexico in about 25 years.

Why it's cool: a quarter century of absence, ended by a patient student and a folder of trail-cam photos.

Novo tipo de escova de dentes usa cargas elétricas para eliminar bactérias

Superinteressante

Desde pelo menos o século 15, escovar os dentes é basicamente uma questão de atrito. Pesquisadores da Universidade de Pequim acrescentaram um truque: cerdas que geram uma pequena carga elétrica enquanto você escova, e essa eletricidade ataca as bactérias que formam a placa.

Depois de três meses de uso, os voluntários tiveram menos placa, menos inflamação e menos sangramento na gengiva do que quem usou cerdas comuns de náilon. Custo e segurança a longo prazo ainda precisam ser avaliados.

Por que a vontade de fazer xixi aumenta quando chegamos perto de casa?

Superinteressante · Mundo Estranho

Você vem tranquilo pela rua e, assim que a chave entra na fechadura, a vontade vira urgência. O fenômeno tem até apelido em inglês, latchkey urgency, e é disparado por pistas do ambiente que o cérebro associa ao banheiro, como chegar em casa ou ouvir água correndo.

Ainda é pouco estudado: um dos principais trabalhos, de 2017, ouviu 328 mulheres e viu que o efeito é mais forte em quem tem bexiga hiperativa, mas aparece também em quem não tem.

Por que é legal: é Pavlov, só que no corredor de casa.

What Else Could It Mean? A Book Surveys the Playful, Iconoclastic Works of James Wines

Colossal

Since 1970, James Wines and his New York studio SITE have treated buildings as jokes, sculptures and questions all at once. Their famous BEST Products showrooms had facades that seemed to crumble, peel away or sink into the ground, and their "Ghost Parking Lot" buried twenty cars under asphalt.

A new Skira book gathers five decades of Wines' drawings, renderings and writings alongside photos of the built work.

Why it's cool: proof that architecture can be "often ironic but never cynical."

Aminah Brenda Lynn Robinson's Whimsical Books and Dolls Celebrate Black Community

Colossal

The Columbus, Ohio artist (1940–2015) stitched the memories of her neighborhood into fabric books and dolls covered with buttons, beads, cowrie shells and thread. She also sculpted with "hogmawg," a mix of clay, mud, twigs, lime and glue taught to her by her father.

Forty of her works go on view in Beaded and Bound in New York from November 19, including a tribute to a local barbershop she saw as a sacred gathering place.

The butterfly effect: a brighter way to make paint?

Positive News

The white of a cabbage white butterfly's wings doesn't come from pigment but from tiny crystals that scatter light. Chemist Leila Deravi's team at Northeastern University is studying these crystals as a recipe for "structural color" paints, tuning the hue by changing crystal size and arrangement.

Such paints could avoid pigments containing lead, cobalt or arsenic and might resist fading in sunlight. The catch: so far they can only grow the crystals by the milligram.

Why it's cool: the next color on your wall might be borrowed from a garden butterfly.

8 de novembro de 1895: Röntgen descobre os raios X

Wikipédia

Numa noite de outono em Würzburg, o físico Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen fazia experimentos com tubos de descarga elétrica quando notou que uma tela fluorescente do outro lado da sala começou a brilhar, mesmo com o tubo coberto. Batizou a radiação misteriosa de "X", por não saber o que era. Semanas depois, fez a famosa imagem da mão da esposa, Anna Bertha, com o anel, e em 1901 recebeu o primeiro Nobel de Física da história.

The Spark: When Communities Own the Land, Housing Stays Affordable

Reasons to be Cheerful

In Smoketown, a historic neighborhood in Louisville, Kentucky, residents worried about rising prices organized a community land trust: a nonprofit that keeps owning the land while families buy the homes on top of it, at lower prices and with a promise to resell affordably.

With around 70 plots set aside and the first seven homes already finished, the piece shows how the model works street by street, including the trust's habit of asking neighbors what they want before building anything. A calm, practical read about people solving a problem together.
