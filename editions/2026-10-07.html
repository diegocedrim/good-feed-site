The first stand-alone nuclear clock is ticking in Vienna

Phys.org

Every clock we use ticks with the help of electrons. A team at TU Wien, working with Germany's PTB, has now built one that keeps time using the nucleus of thorium-229 atoms embedded in a crystal — and, for the first time, it steers its own laser without anyone touching it.

The prototype ran steadily for more than 24 hours. It isn't yet as precise as the best optical atomic clocks, but nuclei are ten thousand times smaller than atoms and far less bothered by the outside world, which is why physicists dream of them as the ultimate timekeepers.

Only two years ago this team first showed thorium nuclei could be nudged by a laser at all. Now it's a working clock.

NASA's Webb finds signs of Mars-sized worlds smashing together

ScienceDaily

Webb looked at 21 unusually dusty disks around young stars and found two flavours: some rich in silica, the fingerprint of really violent, planet-sized collisions, and some poor in it. The silica-rich ones only show up around stars younger than about 300 million years.

That's exactly the age window when rocky planets are thought to be finishing up — and when a Mars-sized body called Theia likely hit the young Earth and made our Moon.

We may be watching, around other stars, the kind of crash that gave us moonlight.

Mars' oddest cloud may be even odder than previously thought

Phys.org

Every Martian spring, a skinny cloud up to 1,800 km long grows from the giant Arsia Mons volcano each morning and vanishes by afternoon. New modelling with Mars Express data suggests it forms in a way never seen in a planet's atmosphere: water vapour freezing straight into ice crystals with no dust speck to start on.

The volcano throws air upward so fast it cools by about 30 degrees in ten minutes, pushing humidity to absurd levels until ice simply appears.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

New crocodile species found fossilized in stone used to build Egypt's Great Sphinx

Mongabay

Fossils dug up in the 1920s and left in a drawer turned out, after CT scans, to be a brand-new 45-million-year-old long-snouted crocodile. It came from the same limestone formation quarried to build the Sphinx — and, like the Sphinx, its fossil is missing the tip of its nose.

Researchers named it Herugavialis cairensis, after the god Heru. Its closest living cousins, the gharials, are endangered, but the article ends on a hopeful note: more than half of crocodilian species are now of "least concern", versus all of them being threatened in 1971.

A crocodile and a Sphinx, both broken-nosed, carved from the same rock.

Scientists changed how zoo lions eat. Their wild side came out

ScienceDaily

At Monarto Safari Park in South Australia, three male lions switched from regular rations to a whole carcass every nine days — feast, then fast, the way wild lions live. They spent nearly three times longer eating, tearing and pulling, then lazed heavily and slowly became more active as the days went on, just like their wild relatives.

A neat surprise: their pacing wasn't tied to hunger at all, but to predictable events like keepers arriving.

Saudi Solar Park Proves Brilliant Breeding Ground for Threatened Sand Gazelles

Good News Network

Inside a huge nature reserve on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast, a fenced 250-megawatt solar farm has become home to 38 Arabian sand gazelles, a species with only about 3,000 left in the wild. The plan is to grow the herd to more than 500.

The secret is the shade: under the panels it's 8 to 18 degrees cooler than in the desert sun, which turns out to be a very comfortable place to raise a family.

Brazilian biologist who stressed snakes until they struck wins Ig Nobel

Jornal da USP

João Miguel Alves-Nunes, from USP's Institute of Biosciences, took home the Ig Nobel in Biology — the prize for research that "first makes people laugh, and then think." His team provoked jararacas, the snake behind most bites in Brazil, in some 40,000 trials to learn what makes them strike.

Size, sex, age, temperature, time of day and where they were touched all mattered. He got bitten four times along the way, and the original paper has since been retracted over paperwork issues, which only adds to the very Ig Nobel flavour of it all.

This Lollipop Could Help Blind Women Independently Detect Pregnancy

Smithsonian Magazine

Home pregnancy tests are purely visual, so blind and low-vision women usually have to ask someone else to read the result. Carnegie Mellon design student Sneha Rao is building Tact, a gelatin lollipop that reacts to the pregnancy hormone in saliva and raises little bumps you can feel with your tongue: both sides for positive, one side for negative.

The physical design is done; next comes the biochemistry and clinical testing. It dissolves after use and leaves no trace — private by design.

Accessibility that's also kind of delightful.

What Are Medieval English Coins Doing in Rome?

Smithsonian Magazine

Digging at a palazzo near the Vatican, archaeologists found 20 tenth-century Anglo-Saxon pennies stamped with the names of English kings. They're physical proof of the Schola Saxonum, a "Little England" quarter that until now was known mostly from old texts.

Traces of leather, bone and metal workshops suggest hundreds of people lived there full time — and so many English coins have turned up in Rome that they outnumber papal ones from the same period, hinting the English pilgrims were allowed to keep their own money.

A Ship Carrying Stacks of Ancient Dishware Sank Off the Coast of Turkey 2,100 Years Ago

Smithsonian Magazine

Off Adrasan, on Turkey's southern coast, divers are recovering thousands of plates, bowls and trays from a ship that went down around the first century B.C.E. They survived in good shape because the ancient packers wedged clay between the pieces, like old-school bubble wrap.

Most of the tableware seems to come from Rhosus, a pottery town so famous that Cicero himself praised its dishes. The team has catalogued 122 different types so far, working 40 to 55 metres down with help from underwater robots.

Do papelão ao acervo da USP: pesquisa acompanha a trajetória das editoras cartoneras

Jornal da USP

As cartoneras fazem livros com capa de papelão reciclado, pintada e costurada à mão — literatura, arte visual e trabalho coletivo no mesmo objeto. O movimento nasceu na Argentina em 2003, com a Eloísa Cartonera, e se espalhou pela América Latina e pelo mundo.

A pesquisadora Ariadne Catarine dos Santos seguiu essa história num doutorado entre a USP e a Sapienza, de Roma, e uma doação formou na Biblioteca Florestan Fernandes o primeiro acervo de cartoneras numa biblioteca acadêmica brasileira. Por aqui, a Dulcinéia Catadora, formada por catadoras de recicláveis, já lançou mais de 150 títulos.

Cada exemplar é único — nenhuma capa sai igual à outra.

Precarious Dwellings Perch Atop Rocks in Simon Laveuve's 1/35-Scale Miniatures

Colossal

French artist Simon Laveuve builds tiny homes clinging to boulders and stilts, furnished with salvaged odds and ends, little daily objects, and every sign that someone just stepped out.

At 1/35 scale, they're the kind of pieces you lean in close to, hunting for the next detail.

Hannah Lim Draws on Her British-Singaporean Heritage in Sculptural Snuff Bottles

Colossal

London-based Hannah Lim takes the small, ornate snuff bottle and blows it up into big ceramic and polymer-clay sculptures. Her pieces play with 17th- and 18th-century Chinoiserie and creatures from the ancient Chinese bestiary The Classic of Mountains and Seas.

The result is playful and dense — a pocket-sized object turned into a whole world of mixed heritage.

Wikipédia

Em 7 de outubro de 1959, a sonda Luna 3 fotografou pela primeira vez o lado da Lua que nunca fica voltado para a Terra. As imagens eram granuladas, reveladas a bordo e transmitidas por rádio — mas mostraram uma face bem diferente, quase sem os grandes "mares" escuros que a gente vê daqui.
