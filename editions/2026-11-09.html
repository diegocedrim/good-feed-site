🔬 Ciência & Espaço

CHAPEA Mission 2 Crew Reflects After 378 Days on Simulated Mars

NASA

On October 31, four volunteers walked out of Mars Dune Alpha, a 3D-printed habitat inside NASA's Johnson Space Center, after more than a year living as if they were on the Red Planet. They grew food, ran "Marswalks," dealt with communication delays and limited supplies, and had only each other for company.

Now they're talking about what got them through: teamwork, routines and patience. Their health and performance data will help plan real crews for the Moon and Mars.

Why it's cool: a year of everyday life, rehearsed for another planet.

A hummingbird's brilliant magenta feathers depend on a hidden middle layer

Phys.org

The magenta on a male Anna's hummingbird isn't pigment: it's structure. Tiny stacks of melanin granules and air pockets reflect red and blue light that overlap into magenta, but nobody could fully explain where the blue came from.

A team at Yonsei University found an overlooked layer of medium-sized structures between the surface and the deeper ones. When they etched away the outer surface with plasma, the blue vanished and the feather turned orange.

Why it's cool: the bird's color is basically nano-engineering.

JWST tracks more than 100 magnified stellar events in the Dragon galaxy

Phys.org

The "Dragon" is a distant galaxy stretched into a long arc by the gravity of a cluster in front of it, which works like a giant natural lens. Within that arc, individual stars occasionally flare into view when they're magnified even more.

Using the James Webb telescope, astronomers counted 104 of these brief events, more than double the 44 known before. Their pattern can reveal the bright stars of the far universe and may even hint at how dark matter is distributed.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Glowworms Shine Again in the UK from Larvae Reared Pro Bono in Garden Shed

Good News Network

Glowworms hadn't been seen at Nosterfield Nature Reserve in North Yorkshire for about a century. Then Peter Cooper, a local wildlife lover, started breeding them in his garden shed in his spare time.

He's counted 1,900 larvae ready to be released this year. The real test comes later: success means their wild-born offspring glowing on their own, perhaps around 2030.

Why it's cool: a patient hobby that may relight a whole meadow.

100 buses long: Scientists map one of the world's largest known coral colonies

Phys.org

Off the remote Cocos (Keeling) Islands in the Indian Ocean, a rose coral stretches about 1.56 km along the reef slope, at depths from 14 to 65 meters, covering an area of nearly 57,000 m².

Researchers mapped it over 12 dives with GPS; a local dive operator had first noticed pieces of it more than 30 years ago. Genetic tests will now show whether it's one giant individual or several colonies that fused.

Em iniciativa inédita, cientistas compilam e compartilham informações sobre 2 mil espécies de plantas do Cerrado

Superinteressante · Jornal da Unesp

Pesquisadores da Unesp e da UFMG juntaram o trabalho de mais de cem cientistas num banco de dados aberto, o Cerrado Plant Traits: mais de 100 mil registros sobre cerca de 2 mil espécies, com 154 características de folhas, raízes, flores, frutos e sementes.

A ideia é dar à savana mais diversa do mundo o espaço que ela merece nos bancos globais e ajudar quem planeja projetos de restauração do bioma.

Por que é legal: ciência brasileira coletiva, aberta para qualquer um usar.

Adventurer Finally Hugs Mom After 28-Year Walk Around the World From Home in UK

Good News Network

In 1998, Karl Bushby left Hull, England, determined to walk around the world and come home without ever using motorized transport. About 31,000 miles and some 30 countries later, his mother was waiting for him in tears.

Along the way he crossed the Darién Gap, walked across the frozen Bering Strait in 2006, swam more than 170 miles across the Caspian Sea and, at the end, swam the English Channel.

Why it's cool: proof that "one step at a time" really can go all the way around.

🛠️ Tecnologia, História & Curiosidades

CT scanning the centuries-old head of Jeremy Bentham

Phys.org

The philosopher Jeremy Bentham asked to be preserved after death as an "auto-icon": his skeleton, dressed in his own clothes, still sits at University College London, topped by a wax head. The real head is kept in a conservation safe.

A UCL team has now CT-scanned it. The images confirm what his letters complained about, badly damaged teeth and an arthritic jaw, and reveal his glass eyes in detail. The scans can also be used to 3D-print his skull.

Why it's cool: an 1832 toothache, diagnosed with 2026 technology.

Where Did Stonehenge's Mysterious, Six-Ton Altar Stone Really Come From?

Smithsonian Magazine

Stonehenge's Altar Stone is a roughly six-ton slab of sandstone, and a 2024 study traced it to northeast Scotland, hundreds of kilometers away. A new geological analysis says the composition doesn't actually match.

So the mystery is open again, and researchers are heading back into the field to find where Neolithic builders really got their stone.

O que é gelo seco e como ele é feito?

Superinteressante

Gelo-seco não tem água: é gás carbônico em estado sólido, que passa direto para gás sem derreter, por isso não molha nada. A matéria-prima muitas vezes é o CO₂ que sobra de processos industriais, como a produção de amônia.

O gás é resfriado e comprimido até virar líquido. Quando é solto de repente à pressão normal, metade vira gás e metade vira uma neve finíssima, que depois é prensada em blocos ou bastõezinhos.

A trip to upside-down world

Aeon · video

Part of MoMA's "Once Upon an Artwork" series, this short film lets two children, Dili and Amalia, step into Marc Chagall's 1911 painting I and the Village, his dreamlike memory of childhood in Vitebsk.

In their animated version, day and night happen at the same time, memories become visible and everything can turn upside down.

Why it's cool: a painting seen the way kids see it, which is probably how Chagall meant it.

Fuente de los Niños Miones

Atlas Obscura · Guadalajara, Mexico

In Plaza Tapatía, in Guadalajara's historic center, sculptor Miguel Miramontes placed four playful child figures in 1982, with water jets spouting from various (yes, cheeky) parts of their bodies.

It sits on the walk between the Degollado Theater and the Cabañas Cultural Institute, and it's the kind of public art that makes passers-by laugh out loud.

Ideas from chaotic systems lead him to gentle conclusions: resilience comes from leaving some slack instead of always running at the limit, and physics at its best teaches humility and better questions. A thoughtful, warm read for a slow moment.
