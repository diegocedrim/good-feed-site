NASA spots a giant patch of Moon-like rock on Earth

ScienceDaily

From the International Space Station, an astronaut with a 400 mm lens photographed the Saririaky massif in southern Madagascar: about 100 square kilometres of anorthosite, the same pale rock that forms the bright highlands you see on the Moon.

Anorthosite forms when molten rock cools slowly underground and grows large crystals. On the Moon it dates from a time over 4 billion years ago when the surface was an ocean of magma; Madagascar's version is "only" 600 million years old and records the continental collisions that built the supercontinent Gondwana.

Next time you look at the Moon's bright patches, there's a piece of them sitting in Madagascar.

ALMA catches a dying star mid-transformation, with a ring from its last outburst

Phys.org

Stars like the Sun don't just switch off: they puff out their outer layers and, for a brief cosmic moment, become a "preplanetary nebula". A team led by Raghvendra Sahai at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory used the ALMA radio telescopes in Chile to look at one of these, IRAS 06530, caught in the middle of the change.

They found a large circular ring left by the star's last outburst, curved filaments holding most of the mass, and a doughnut-shaped torus that formed within only the last 700 years. Oddly, there were none of the fast jets that models predict — a hint that something else is sculpting these shapes.

A snapshot of a star's final chapter, in progress.

Método ‘verde’ usa solvente à base de açúcar para extrair bioativos da casca do guaraná

Agência FAPESP

De cada fruto de guaraná, de 80% a 85% costumam ir para o lixo. Pesquisadores da Unicamp criaram um jeito de aproveitar a casca das sementes usando um solvente feito de 1,3-propanodiol, glicose e ácido cítrico, no lugar de solventes tóxicos como o metanol.

A mistura tira cafeína e antioxidantes da casca, e o extrato já sai pronto para uso industrial, sem a etapa final de purificação — o que economiza tempo e energia.

Química com ingredientes de cozinha, valorizando um fruto bem brasileiro.

Giant Tortoise Population is Thriving – Survey Finds 180,000 on One Island Chain

Good News Network

In the 19th century, fewer than 1,000 giant tortoises were left on Aldabra Atoll, in the Seychelles. A survey by the Seychelles Islands Foundation and the University of Exeter, using counts from 2018 to 2022, now estimates around 180,000 — the highest number ever recorded.

The atoll became a strict nature reserve in 1981 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site a year later. Invasive rats and cats and future droughts are still risks, but today the tortoises comfortably outnumber the people of the whole country.

Slow and steady, quite literally: four decades of protection paid off.

Researcher who listens to forests wins 2026 Pritzker Environmental Genius Award

Mongabay

Zuzana Burivalova, a tropical forest ecologist at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, runs the Sound Forest Lab: recorders left in forests from Papua New Guinea and Indonesia to Peru, Madagascar and Ecuador capture the calls of birds, frogs, insects and mammals.

Those soundscapes become a baseline of what a healthy forest sounds like, so changes can be heard over time. She'll use the $100,000 prize to bring more local scientists and communities into the project.

Taking a forest's pulse just by listening to it.

Italian rice farmer beats heat with biodiversity haven

Phys.org

On the Vercelli plain between Milan and Turin, Alice Cerutti has turned about a third of her 110-hectare rice farm, Cascina Oschiena, back into woodland and wetland, planting more than 10,000 native trees.

Butterflies and dragonflies came back in force, along with rarities like the large copper butterfly and the black-tailed godwit. She grows heritage rice with green manure and grazing sheep instead of chemical fertiliser, and says the old varieties handle drought and heat better.

A farm that makes room for wildlife — and gets tougher for it.

Good News Network

The state expects to take it over in late 2027 and open it for camping, kayaking, hiking and wildlife watching. "This land is part of my soul," said Sam Pollard — and now it will be part of a lot of other people's weekends too.

A One-of-a-Kind Bronze Ring Offers a Glimpse at the Lighthouse of Alexandria

Smithsonian Magazine

The Lighthouse of Alexandria was one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, but nothing of it stands today, so every ancient image of it is precious. Archaeologists excavating Antirhodos, an island in Alexandria's ancient harbour, have found a bronze signet ring engraved with the tower.

It's rare visual evidence of what one of antiquity's most famous buildings looked like — carried around on someone's finger.

A lost wonder, preserved in miniature on someone's hand.

'DNA Typewriter' records cells' history in developing mouse embryo

Phys.org

How does one fertilised egg become a whole body, and which cell came from which? A team led by Jay Shendure at the University of Washington placed a "DNA Typewriter" into a mouse egg's genome: each time cells divide, it types a new mark into the DNA, in order, without cutting it.

Reading those marks later, the researchers rebuilt the family tree of a mouse embryo's cells across its first 13.5 days, from two cells to forming organs. A map like this could help explain birth defects, genetic disorders and cancer.

Every cell carries its own diary, typed one letter per division.

12 de outubro de 1931: inaugurado o Cristo Redentor

Wikipédia

Há 95 anos, o Rio ganhava o seu cartão-postal no alto do Corcovado. Foram nove anos de obra: o engenheiro Heitor da Silva Costa concebeu a estrutura, o escultor franco-polonês Paul Landowski fez a figura e o romeno Gheorghe Leonida, o rosto. São 30 metros de altura e 28 de braços abertos, em concreto armado coberto por um mosaico de pequenas peças de pedra-sabão. Em 2007, foi eleito uma das sete maravilhas do mundo moderno.

Is everything conscious?

Aeon · Sam Kimpton-Nye

Philosopher Sam Kimpton-Nye, of the University of Southampton, starts from an old and very reasonable demand: that everything should have an explanation. Follow that idea far enough, he shows, and you arrive at panpsychism — the view that some form of experience runs all the way down to basic matter. He walks through three routes there, from the "hard problem" of consciousness to Spinoza's idea of a single substance.

Then he steps back and asks whether a fully explicable world is even something we should want — leaving room for philosophy as a way of seeing more, not of closing every question. About 29 minutes of reading, perfect for a slow holiday morning.
