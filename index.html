🔬 Ciência & Espaço

Scientists just found dinosaur blood that survived 115 million years

ScienceDaily

Inside the limb bones of Aratasaurus museunacionali, a meat-eating dinosaur from northeastern Brazil, researchers found mineralized red blood cells tucked into the bone's tiny vascular canals. Chemical tests picked up heme, the iron-carrying part of hemoglobin, apparently preserved thanks to a mineral called hausmannite and a low-oxygen burial.

The team, led by Curtin University with Brazil's Museu Nacional and the Universidade Regional do Cariri, published the work in PNAS. It's the first chemical confirmation of hemoglobin-derived molecules inside fossil dinosaur blood cells.

Why it's cool: the dinosaur's name honors the Museu Nacional, and now its bones are rewriting what "deep time" can preserve.

Rubin Observatory discovers two hidden companions of the Milky Way. Hundreds more may be waiting

ScienceDaily

Using only preview data covering about 7% of the sky, astronomers working with the new Vera C. Rubin Observatory spotted two previously unknown neighbors: Aquarius IV, a tiny, faint galaxy some 355,000 light-years away, and Rubin-GC1, a star cluster hiding behind thick dust on the far side of our galaxy.

About 65 satellite galaxies of the Milky Way are known so far. Rubin's 10-year survey has only just started, and estimates suggest it could reveal hundreds more.

Why it's cool: our cosmic neighborhood turns out to be far more crowded than the map says.

Strange Gas in Mars' Atmosphere Hints the Red Planet Is Still Volcanically Active

Smithsonian Magazine

Mars is usually described as a geologically quiet world, but a new study proposes that fissures on one of its big volcanoes may still be leaking magmatic gases. If that holds up, the planet's interior isn't as dead as assumed.

The authors go a step further: cracks fed by underground heat could host conditions similar to Earth's hydrothermal vents, which makes them attractive targets in the search for life.

Scientists discovered an unknown baby right whale off Canada. They say it's a cause for joy

Mongabay

North Atlantic right whales are so rare that researchers try to track every single calf. Yet one big youngster slipped past everyone for months, until it was photographed in September off Quebec's Anticosti Island.

The surprise baby brings the count from the latest calving season to 24, a welcome number for a species that scientists follow almost one individual at a time.

Why it's cool: in a species this small, one extra calf really does change the story.

These divers are killing a beautiful coral to save Brazil's marine life

Mongabay · vídeo

Sun coral, a bright orange Indo-Pacific species, hitched a ride to Brazil on an oil platform in the 1980s and has since spread along roughly 3,500 km of coast, crowding out native corals and even releasing chemicals that harm them.

Around the Cagarras Islands, just off Ipanema, ICMBio marine biologist Adriana Gomes leads volunteer divers who pry the colonies off the rocks one by one to give the native reef room to breathe.

Why it's cool: a little patience and a lot of dives, right in Rio's backyard.

These Moths Use Their Genitals to Fend Off Bats—but Not in the Way You Think

Smithsonian Magazine

Bats hunt by sonar, and some male hawkmoths fight back by making their own ultrasound, including with structures on their genitals, to jam the bats' echolocation.

The new research found that these moths carry three separate sound-making systems, which seem to have evolved independently several times over the last 5 to 30 million years.

Why it's cool: evolution kept reinventing the same trick, a sonar jammer, in different parts of the body.

Só Notícia Boa

Thiago Santos Ferreira, 18 anos, mora na zona rural de Água Fria e estuda no CETEP Sisal, em Serrinha (BA). Ele criou o Biocimento, uma mistura que aproveita papel usado e fibra de coco junto com o cimento comum para fazer blocos ecológicos de calçada.

Adventurer Finally Hugs Mom After 28-Year Walk Around the World From Home in UK

Good News Network

In 1998, Karl Bushby left Hull, England, with a simple rule: get back home without using any transport. Over 28 years and more than 31,000 miles across 30 countries, he crossed the Darién Gap, walked over the frozen Bering Strait in 2006, and swam about a month across the Caspian Sea.

The last obstacle was the English Channel, which he swam over three days. On November 2 he walked the final three miles into Hull and hugged his mother, Angela, at her garden gate.

Why it's cool: it's the longest unbroken journey on foot ever recorded, and it ended with a hug from mum.

Man Spends 12 Days Returning Lost Cash a Laborer Needed for His Daughter

Good News Network

Pedro Moro found a stack of reais in a plaza in Ibiraçu, Espírito Santo. Nobody came back for it, so he posted on social media, asked around, and finally found a shop whose security camera showed who had dropped it.

The owner, 24-year-old construction worker Matheus Reginaldo Celestino, had lost his entire monthly salary, meant for his daughter. Moro checked he was the right person by asking how many 10, 20 and 100 notes were in the pile, then handed it all back.

Why it's cool: twelve days of detective work, just because it was the right thing to do.

This Wooden Wardrobe in Pompeii Remained Sealed for Nearly 2,000 Years. Archaeologists Just Opened It for the First Time

Smithsonian Magazine

A wooden cabinet buried by Vesuvius stayed closed for about two millennia, and archaeologists have finally looked inside. It held the ordinary stuff of a Roman household: tools, a perfume bottle, an incense burner, pottery and a necklace strung with at least 24 amulets.

Finds like this are less about treasure than about daily life, showing what one family kept close at hand and maybe what they hoped would protect them.

Why it's cool: it's basically opening someone's junk drawer from the year 79.

For Centuries, Cave Visitors Walked Right by 280 Ice Age Engravings

Smithsonian Magazine

People have been walking through a cave in Catalonia for centuries without noticing what was scratched into its walls: 280 Ice Age engravings of animals and abstract shapes, now finally documented by researchers.

Catalonia has very little Paleolithic rock art, so this single cave substantially changes the picture of who lived there and what they drew.

A cidade soterrada de Acrotíri: conheça a "versão grega" de Pompeia

Superinteressante

Em 1867, operários que extraíam pedra-pomes em Santorini para as obras do Canal de Suez esbarraram em construções enterradas sob as cinzas. Era Acrotíri, cidade da Idade do Bronze com ruas pavimentadas, drenagem sofisticada e casas de dois e três andares.

Lá dentro, afrescos de dois meninos boxeando, andorinhas entre flores e uma frota de navios em miniatura. A cidade foi coberta pela enorme erupção de Thera, há mais de 3.500 anos, e, ao contrário de Pompeia, quase não se acharam restos humanos: tudo indica que os moradores tiveram tempo de sair.

Por que é legal: uma Pompeia mil anos mais velha, descoberta por acaso graças a um canal no Egito.

A Strange Quantum Theorem Has Killed Einstein's Reality

Quanta Magazine · Charlie Wood · ~2.500 palavras

Einstein hoped the quantum wave function was like a weather forecast: a statement about what we know, sitting on top of a perfectly ordinary reality. In 2012, Matthew Pusey, Jonathan Barrett and Terry Rudolph proved a theorem (PBR) showing that, under reasonable assumptions, that comfortable middle ground doesn't exist.

Charlie Wood walks through the line from Einstein's 1935 argument to Bell and then PBR, and what physicists are left with: either a reality that is objective but very strange, or no objective reality at all. A beautiful, mind-bending read for a weekend morning.
