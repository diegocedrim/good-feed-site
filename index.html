The eye has a hidden cleanup system scientists never knew existed

ScienceDaily

For more than a century, textbooks said the eye had no connection to the lymphatic system, the body's network for draining fluid and waste. A team from the University of British Columbia and the University of Toronto, led by Neeru Gupta and Yeni Yücel, has now found one hiding in the choroid, the layer just beneath the retina.

Tracking fluorescent tracers in mice with MRI and near-infrared imaging, they watched fluid leave the back of the eye and reach nearby lymph nodes within minutes. They named the route POLO (posterior ocular lymphatic outflow), and it could become a new target for treating glaucoma and macular degeneration.

A plumbing system hiding in plain sight, in one of the most-studied organs we have.

Vacina 100% brasileira contra malária é liberada para testes em humanos

Só Notícia Boa

A Vivaxin, criada por pesquisadores da UFMG com a USP e o laboratório Cristália, mira o Plasmodium vivax, parasita responsável por cerca de 85% dos casos de malária no Brasil. As duas vacinas que existem no mundo protegem só contra o P. falciparum, mais comum na África — então esta pode ser a primeira contra o vivax.

A primeira fase vai acompanhar 48 voluntários de 18 a 59 anos no Hospital das Clínicas da UFMG, em Belo Horizonte, testando três doses diferentes ao longo de quase um ano.

Ciência feita aqui, para um problema que é muito daqui — e que pode ajudar o mundo todo.

Enzima encontrada na raiz da cana aumenta em seis vezes a produção de açúcar a partir do bagaço

Agência FAPESP

A raiz da cana “desmonta” parte das próprias células para abrir canais porosos chamados aerênquimas. Estudando esse processo por 17 anos, a equipe de Marcos Buckeridge, da USP, encontrou a enzima que faz esse trabalho — e ela multiplica por até seis a quantidade de açúcar extraída do bagaço.

Até hoje, as enzimas usadas para quebrar fibras vegetais vinham de fungos e bactérias. É a primeira vez que se aponta uma de origem vegetal com esse poder, o que pode ajudar o etanol de segunda geração, feito de resíduos.

A planta já sabia fazer sozinha o que a indústria tenta fazer há décadas.

Zimbabwe's dinosaurs were different from South Africa's: New species offers clues

Phys.org

Meet Musango matusadonaensis, a long-necked plant-eater that lived about 210 million years ago, in the Late Triassic. Its partial skeleton was found along the Nyamasango River in Zimbabwe by a team led by Jonah Choiniere of the University of the Witwatersrand.

The surprise is that Zimbabwe's dinosaurs of that time look distinct from those just south, in South Africa. The researchers suspect climate: Zimbabwe sat closer to the equator, and different weather may have shaped different animals.

Even 210 million years ago, neighbours had their own local fauna.

Neanderthals may have used prehistoric glue as an ancient antibiotic

ScienceDaily

Neanderthals made birch tar by heating birch bark, and archaeologists have long known they used it as glue to fix stone points to handles. Researchers from Cologne, Oxford, Liège and Cape Breton recreated the tar using prehistoric methods and tested it against Staphylococcus aureus, a common cause of wound infections.

Every batch held the bacteria back, no matter how it was made. That doesn't prove Neanderthals put it on wounds, but it makes the idea of a prehistoric first-aid kit a lot more plausible.

The same sticky stuff may have held both their tools and their health together.

NASA Demonstrates Next-Generation Heat Shield Technologies

NASA

When a Northrop Grumman cargo ship left the Space Station and burned up on reentry, it didn't go alone: it released 12 small capsules built with University of Kentucky students, each carrying a different experimental heat shield.

The prototypes ranged from ceramic tiles and 3D-printed shields to flexible carbon-fiber fabric and umbrella-like designs meant for landing on Mars or Saturn's moon Titan. Using a spacecraft's final plunge this way gives real flight data for materials that are still on the drawing board.

Even a spacecraft's last minutes get put to good use.

Where a land ethic blooms

Aeon · Craig Maier

Craig Maier was a prairie lover who worked with fire science for Wisconsin's tallgrass prairies and oak savannas, and also apprenticed on his father-in-law's farm. In this essay he asks how a farm can feed people while also making room for prairie, birds and wild neighbours.

His answer is that a "land ethic" isn't a set of rules you read somewhere; it grows out of years of working one place and the people around it — and love sits at the heart of good farming and conservation alike. Maier died in 2024, which makes the piece feel even more like a gift. About 29 minutes of reading.
