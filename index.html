🔬 Ciência & Espaço

Europa's sodium cloud comes from its hidden ocean

Phys.org

Jupiter's moon Europa is wrapped in a faint cloud of sodium stretching up to 30 times its own radius. Using Keck Observatory data going back to 1999, a Boston University team found that cloud has stayed remarkably steady for over two decades — it doesn't rise and fall with the eruptions of neighboring volcanic moon Io. The conclusion: the salt is leaking out from Europa's own subsurface ocean. The trick was to observe Europa while it sat in Jupiter's shadow, so its bright ice wouldn't drown out the glow.

Por que é legal: estamos “provando” o sal de um oceano escondido sob o gelo, a 600 milhões de km.

Scientists turn Moon dust into LEGO-like bricks

ScienceDaily

A University of Arkansas team, led by an undergraduate, used a relatively low-power laser to fuse simulated lunar soil layer by layer into small interlocking bricks shaped like crosses and H's — no glue or binder needed from Earth. The pieces stand on their own but are still too porous to carry heavy loads, and the process must yet be tested in vacuum and low gravity.

Why it's cool: a future Moon base might be snapped together like a toy set, made from the ground under the astronauts' feet.

Molécula estudada por brasileiros pode proteger a visão de quem tem diabetes

Superinteressante (via Agência FAPESP)

Pesquisadores da Unifesp e da Unesp, com colegas da Universidade do Colorado, testaram o peptídeo Ac2-26 — um pedaço de uma proteína anti-inflamatória que o próprio corpo produz — contra a retinopatia diabética, que afeta mais de 1 em cada 5 pessoas com diabetes. Em células de retina e em “minirretinas” cultivadas a partir de células-tronco de pacientes, a molécula reduziu a inflamação, protegeu as mitocôndrias e impediu a morte dos neurônios expostos a muito açúcar.

Por que é legal: ciência paulista mirando uma das principais causas de cegueira evitável.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

A seaweed unseen for 90 years — hiding in vast underwater forests

Phys.org

The seaweed Sargassum howeanum was described near Australia's Lord Howe Island in 1935, but for decades only small, immature bits turned up in shallow water. Then underwater video in 2022 revealed dense forests of it at 20–38 metres deep, with adult plants up to 72 cm long — and the area covered grew 22-fold between 2009 and 2022. Scientists suspect cooler deep waters could serve as refuges for seaweed as the oceans warm.

Why it's cool: a “lost” species wasn't gone — it was just living a little deeper than anyone looked.

Arara que se afogava no rio “agradece” ao barqueiro que a salvou na Amazônia

Só Notícia Boa

Um piloteiro estendeu o remo para uma arara que se debatia na água de um rio amazônico, e ela subiu no barco. Com uma asa machucada, a ave foi levada até uma ilha para se recuperar numa árvore — não sem antes encostar o bico no salvador, num gesto que parece um obrigado. O turista Pedro Paulo Brisque, de Sumaré (SP), filmou tudo, e o vídeo passou de meio milhão de visualizações.

Thin mountain air may protect honey bees from a deadly mite

Phys.org

Chinese researchers placed 60 mite-infested hives at sites across Tibet and Yunnan. In Lhasa, above 3,500 metres, 13 of 15 colonies survived two years — while hives elsewhere didn't make it past one. The Varroa mites simply reproduced less up high, and recreating low-oxygen conditions inside lowland hives had the same effect.

Why it's cool: a possible chemical-free trick against one of the bees' worst enemies.

Rhino dung paper: how a father and daughter turned poop into protection

Good News Network

In Assam, India, retired mining engineer Mahesh Chandra Bora and his daughter Nisha run Elrhino, which makes notebooks, lampshades and playing cards from rhino and elephant dung — the first tests were done in his wife's kitchen blender. The dung is washed and boiled to recover plant fibres, then pulped with recycled paper. Villagers near Kaziranga and Pobitora parks are paid to collect it, giving them income that offsets crop damage from wandering rhinos.

Why it's cool: it turns the rhino from a nuisance into a neighbour worth keeping around.

Every Tuesday, this chef cooks for 100 people with whatever the food bank has

Good News Network

Toronto chef Maddy Goldberg left restaurant kitchens — where she watched huge amounts of food go to waste — and now volunteers weekly at the Fort York Food Bank. She improvises a real meal from the week's random donations, like lentil fritters with caramelized onion, cinnamon and curry. Her videos of the mystery-box cooking have drawn 180,000 followers.

🛠️ Tecnologia, História & Curiosidades

Montreal is using soothing ocean sounds to soften traffic noise

Reasons to be Cheerful

A small park beside a noisy junction in Verdun plays waves, birdsong and crunching sand recorded on the Magdalen Islands — home of the families who settled in that neighbourhood. Designers tuned the frequencies so the waves wouldn't be mistaken for cars, and cut boat horns because people didn't find them relaxing. The installation is now permanent, and next year residents will help choose the sounds.

Why it's cool: instead of just blocking city noise, it designs what the city sounds like.

Have archaeologists finally pieced together the Parthenon puzzle?

Smithsonian Magazine

The Parthenon's east pediment once showed the birth of Athena, but most of its sculptures were lost centuries ago. German researchers now believe they have reconstructed the full 39-figure scene, using Roman sarcophagi that appear to copy it as clues and 3D printing to test the arrangement.

Why it's cool: a 2,400-year-old jigsaw, solved with Roman “photocopies” and modern printers.

Qual é a origem do patinho de borracha?

Superinteressante

Os primeiros brinquedos de borracha surgiram depois que Charles Goodyear inventou a vulcanização, em 1839 — mas eram pesados e serviam de mordedor para bebês (e cachorros). O patinho amarelo como conhecemos ganhou forma em 1948, em Hong Kong, e no ano seguinte o artista americano Peter Ganine patenteou a versão flutuante e simpática que vendeu milhões. Detalhe: a maioria dos “patinhos de borracha” é, na verdade, de vinil.

Dramatic waves crash on Iceland's coast in photos by Vilhelm Gunnarsson

Colossal

Gunnarsson, a press photographer who also volunteers on a rescue team, heads to the volcanic Reykjanes Peninsula during or right after storms, when the surf is wildest. His black-and-white images show waves exploding on black rocks, rolling onto black-sand beaches seen from above, and sunlight breaking through over the dark Atlantic — in one crest, he even sees a ghostly face.

A tiny taxi, a pint-size cookstove and 300 more marvelous miniatures

Smithsonian Magazine

“Mighty Miniatures,” a new show at the National Museum of the American Indian in New York, gathers more than 300 small-scale works by Native artists from across the Americas and Hawaii. The pieces range from everyday objects — a miniature taxi, a little cookstove — to traditional crafts shrunk down with astonishing detail.

Why it's cool: proof that you don't need size to tell a big story.

20 de novembro de 1889: nasce Edwin Hubble

Wikipédia

Nascido no Missouri, Hubble mostrou nos anos 1920 que as “nebulosas espirais” eram galáxias inteiras além da Via Láctea — e que o universo está se expandindo. De repente, o cosmos ficou incomparavelmente maior. Hoje, o telescópio espacial com seu nome continua a nos mostrar esse universo, e vai procurar magnésio na atmosfera de Europa, a lua da primeira história de hoje.

My grandmother was a seal

Aeon · ensaio de Jordan Lee Borgman · ~3.700 palavras

Many Irish families once claimed descent from selkies — seals who shed their skins to live as women, often kept ashore because a husband hid the sealskin. Starting from folklore recordings made in 1948, Borgman argues these tales, with their theft and abandonment, offer a more honest picture of our kinship with animals than today's sentimental “pets are family” talk. A beautiful, melancholy essay about how love and loss can share the same shoreline.
