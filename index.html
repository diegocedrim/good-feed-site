Physicists create a bizarre new quantum state: a "fractional Fermi sea"

ScienceDaily

A team at the University of Innsbruck, led by Hanns-Christoph Nägerl, lined up ultracold cesium atoms in one-dimensional tubes and then kept flipping their interactions back and forth, from strongly repelling to strongly attracting. Instead of dissolving into chaos, the atoms settled into a state that is highly excited and yet strangely orderly.

Existing theories of one-dimensional quantum matter don't fully explain what they saw, and the team suspects new quasiparticles are involved, which they've nicknamed "super-fermions" for now.

Quantum simulators are no longer just reproducing known physics: they are finding states nobody predicted.

Japan's MMX mission is about to set off to grab soil from Mars's moon Phobos

Phys.org

Launch is set for tomorrow, 19 October, from Tanegashima on an H3 rocket. The spacecraft should reach Mars in August 2027, orbit Phobos, drop off a small rover called Idefix, touch down itself in 2030, and bring at least 10 grams of moon dust back to Earth in 2031.

The big question is where Mars's two little moons came from: were they passing asteroids captured by the planet's gravity, or debris thrown up by a giant impact on Mars? The chemistry of the samples should tell.

The first sample ever brought back from the Martian system could come from a potato-shaped moon.

Plants change their gene activity within minutes of reaching space

Phys.org

University of Florida biologists Rob Ferl and Anna-Lisa Paul flew small Arabidopsis plants on a short suborbital hop, with Ferl himself on board to "freeze" samples at four moments: before launch, on reaching weightlessness, during descent and after landing. Identical plants on the ground were preserved at the same times for comparison.

More than 1,200 genes changed their activity, with roots and leaves responding differently at each stage. Adaptation to space, it turns out, is not one switch but a sequence of quick reactions.

Close friends help wild jackdaws keep their spot at the feeder, even if it means waiting

Phys.org

Researchers tracked wild jackdaws at feeding stations and found the birds strongly preferred to show up with their bonded partners: mates and close relatives. Together, they were much less likely to be shoved away by other birds.

Friendship has a price, though. Partners often waited their turn so the other could eat first. And young jackdaws tagging along with their parents may be learning how to use the feeders, an extra perk of staying close to family.

Even in a crowd of crows, it pays to have someone who saves you a place in line.

Free-roaming bison on the American Prairie pass 1,000 for the first time

Good News Network

After more than 20 years of work, the nonprofit American Prairie now has 1,016 bison roaming its land in Montana. The group manages about 600,000 acres and hopes to stitch together 3.2 million, which would put it among the ten largest protected areas in the US.

Bison today number only 1–3.5% of what once lived on the plains, and few herds get to move around freely. Every animal added to a wild-roaming herd matters.

Regeneração natural de florestas ajuda a conter o avanço de espécies invasoras

Agência FAPESP

Um estudo liderado por Angelica Faria de Resende, da Esalq-USP, com pesquisadores de cerca de 60 instituições, analisou 1.561 áreas de floresta secundária em dez países das Américas, do Brasil ao México. Nos primeiros 10 a 20 anos de regeneração, árvores exóticas chegam a ocupar até 28% dos troncos nas matas úmidas.

Mas a vantagem delas dura pouco: essas espécies oportunistas precisam de muita luz e vão sumindo à medida que as nativas crescem e fazem sombra. Ou seja, se deixada em paz, a própria floresta resolve boa parte do problema.

A sombra das árvores nativas é uma ferramenta de manejo gratuita.

Meninas de 14 anos criam ferramenta contra notícias falsas e ganham viagem à Nasa

Só Notícia Boa

Alice Gomes, Beatriz Vasconcellos e Letícia de Oliveira, alunas do 9º ano de um colégio no Recife, criaram a plataforma "Pense Bem". A ideia é simples: antes de compartilhar uma postagem, a pessoa passa o conteúdo pela ferramenta, que aponta sinais de uso de inteligência artificial e ajuda a checar se os fatos se sustentam.

At 99, Helga Duffy becomes the world's oldest female wing-walker

Good News Network

Strapped on top of a biplane over Gloucestershire, England, Helga Duffy spent more than five minutes in the air and earned a Guinness World Record, taking the title from a 97-year-old who had set it just three months earlier. Arthritis and bad knees didn't stop her.

She said the air up there was so pure you wouldn't believe it, and her advice to everyone else is to try anything and never say "I can't". The flight also raised about £1,600 for a local air ambulance.

The record keeps going to women in their late 90s. That says something lovely about the late 90s.

The world's oldest known shipwreck may lie off the coast of Turkey

Phys.org

Divers off the Kekova coast found a wreck whose cargo of large "belted" storage jars points to a date of roughly 2700–2400 B.C. That would make it about 300 years older than the previous record holder, near Dokos in Greece.

The ship may have carried olive oil, wine or tree resin, goods that also show up in ancient Egyptian trade records. The results are still preliminary and not yet peer-reviewed, but they offer a rare look at Bronze Age trade at sea.

Someone loaded those jars more than 4,500 years ago, and they're still sitting where the ship went down.

A hand-cranked gadget reads blood type in about a minute, no electricity needed

Phys.org

Engineers at National Taiwan University built a small disc with tiny channels that you spin with a hand crank, like a mini centrifuge. A few drops of blood from a finger prick flow through channels containing reagents. Where the blood type matches, red cells clump into a visible band, and the length of the band shows how many red cells there are.

Tested on samples from 21 hospital patients, it matched standard lab analyzers closely, however fast or slow the crank was turned. It could be very handy for clinics without power or trained staff.

An English village slows down speeding drivers with knitted traffic cops

Good News Network

After 11 crashes in four years on one road, the local knitting club in Fillongley, Warwickshire, spent four months making life-size wool police officers, complete with hi-vis vests and fake speed guns. They went up on 13 October.

It's working: brake lights come on as soon as drivers spot them. A local councillor noted that people ignore electronic speed signs but slow right down for a woollen officer.

Road safety, powered by a knitting circle.

The Wildlife Photographer of the Year 2026 winners

Colossal

The grand prize went to Italian photographer Federico Veronesi for "The Aftermath", a black-and-white image of an elephant carrying her dead calf. It's a rare, moving glimpse of how animals seem to grieve. In the 10-and-under category, Amelie Davis from the UK won with a common carder bee landing on a flower.

Other highlights include slime moulds, coyotes walking through San Francisco and mushrooms glowing under ultraviolet light. A record 65,403 entries came in from 121 countries, and the top 100 are on show at London's Natural History Museum until July 2027.

Robin F. Williams paints our strange romance with our devices

Colossal

In "Act Natural", a pop-up show in London through 24 October, the Brooklyn painter mixes old art-history poses with glossy, frictionless images of the screen age: someone in bed scrolling inside rainbow infinity loops, a cyborg mermaid, angels holding smartphones.

The paintings are seductive and a little unsettling at the same time, which may be the most honest way to picture how much of our lives now happens on a screen.

18 de outubro de 1989: a sonda Galileu parte para Júpiter

Wikipédia

Há 37 anos, o ônibus espacial Atlantis levava ao espaço a sonda Galileu, a primeira a entrar em órbita de Júpiter. No caminho, ela fez a primeira visita de perto a um asteroide e descobriu que o asteroide Ida tinha a sua própria lua minúscula, Dáctilo.

Ao chegar, em 1995, lançou uma pequena sonda para dentro das nuvens do planeta e passou oito anos estudando suas luas. Os dados dela deram fortes indícios de um oceano escondido sob o gelo de Europa, hoje um dos lugares mais promissores para procurar vida.

How Do Fungi Keep the Planet Healthy?

Quanta Magazine · The Joy of Why (podcast com transcrição completa)

Steven Strogatz and Janna Levin talk with evolutionary biologist Toby Kiers about the fungal networks that live in partnership with plant roots. Through the same thin tubes, these fungi move nutrients up toward plants and carry carbon down into the soil at the same time, which at first seemed physically impossible.

They also seem to "trade": sending more phosphorus to plants that pay more carbon, and less to shaded plants that can't afford it. Together they lock away around 13 billion tons of CO₂ a year. It's about an hour to listen to, or a long, unhurried read in the transcript, perfect for a Sunday.

One network, many genomes: fungi make you question what even counts as an individual.
