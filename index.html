A giant icy world hiding beyond Pluto could be our sixth dwarf planet

ScienceDaily

Astronomers have spotted 2026 ED138, an icy body somewhere between 600 and 1,500 km across, orbiting about 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth — a bit beyond Pluto. It was first picked out in October in data from the new Vera C. Rubin Observatory, then confirmed with 20 more detections and archival observations going back to 2016. If its size holds up, it could become only the sixth officially recognized dwarf planet.

Why it's cool: the solar system still has big, unnamed neighbours waiting at the edge of the map.

A cosmic magnifying glass could reveal the same supernova two more times in roughly 70 years

Phys.org

The James Webb telescope caught a star that exploded 8.8 billion years ago, nicknamed SN Ares, magnified by a massive galaxy cluster acting as a gravitational lens. The cluster bends the light along different paths, so the same explosion should appear twice more — in about 73 and 76 years. Timing those delays will help measure how fast the universe is expanding.

Why it's cool: it's a cosmic rerun already scheduled for our grandchildren.

Como os dinossauros gigantes sustentavam toneladas de peso sem quebrar os ossos?

Superinteressante (Jornal da USP)

Os saurópodes, aqueles herbívoros de pescoço comprido, pesavam dezenas de toneladas, mas seus ossos fossilizados quase nunca mostram fraturas por estresse. Paleontólogos e físicos se juntaram para calcular a pressão nas patas desses animais e descobriram que ela ficava bem abaixo do limite de ruptura. O segredo estaria na arquitetura interna do tecido ósseo, organizada para distribuir a carga.

Por que é legal: o mesmo mineral do nosso esqueleto, arrumado de um jeito engenhoso, aguentava um caminhão em cada passo.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Crows can make decisions twice as fast as humans, scientists discover

ScienceDaily

At the University of Tübingen, carrion crows watched clouds of moving dots on a touchscreen and pecked to say which way most were heading. They decided in about 235 milliseconds — roughly twice as fast as humans and monkeys — with near-perfect accuracy on clear trials. Their brain activity climbed toward a "decision threshold" much like ours does, even though bird brains are built completely differently.

Why it's cool: a very different brain design arrives at the same solution — just faster.

World's largest freshwater lake is recovering after decades of decline

Phys.org

Tree rings from pines around Siberia's Lake Baikal let scientists reconstruct a century of river flow into the lake. The main river feeding it lost up to 90% of its flow in 20th-century droughts, but since 2017 it has surged well above average, with lake levels and vegetation rising too. The researchers credit shifting weather patterns and caution that such rebounds can be short — but for now, the old giant is breathing again.

Why it's cool: the trees kept a diary of the lake's health for a hundred years.

Como um veterinário salvou o animal mais velho do mundo, de 194 anos, cego e morrendo de fome

Superinteressante

Jonathan, uma tartaruga-gigante-das-Seychelles que vive na ilha de Santa Helena, é o animal terrestre mais velho do mundo, com 194 anos. Em 2009, com catarata e o bico se desfazendo, ele mal conseguia comer e parecia perto do fim. O veterinário Joe Hollins passou a alimentá-lo à mão com frutas e verduras — e, dois anos depois, o bico tinha se regenerado e Jonathan estava firme e forte.

Por que é legal: um pouco de paciência e uma cesta de frutas compraram mais de uma década de vida para um bicho que nasceu antes da fotografia.

Só Notícia Boa

Eduardo, aluno do 5º ano de uma escola municipal paulista, melhorou mais de 44% no desempenho e foi homenageado no pátio pela professora Juliana, na frente dos colegas. Couple with Autism and ADHD Find Passion Cleaning Filthy Street Signs

Good News Network

Paul and Ruth Richmond-Watson, a couple from Brighton who are both autistic and have ADHD, found a hobby they love doing together: scrubbing grimy road signs back to life. They clean at least one a day around East Sussex, about 20–25 minutes each, and post the satisfying before-and-afters online. Their dream is a road trip around the UK, leaving a trail of shiny signs behind.

Why it's cool: a small, quiet act of care for something everyone uses and nobody notices.

Taiwan Indigenous farmer revives climate-resilient millet

Phys.org

Banu Ismahasan, a Bunun farmer in southeastern Taiwan, has spent a decade with his elderly parents bringing back "oil millet," a grain his people once grew as insurance against failed harvests. It vanished six decades ago when rice became common, but it needs no irrigation, fertilizer or pesticides — and it stayed green through a drought of more than 200 days. Its seeds are now also kept in a national gene bank for the warmer years ahead.

Why it's cool: an old family crop might be exactly what the future needs.

The world's fastest ocean liner finally hits the ocean floor — and becomes the largest artificial reef

Smithsonian Magazine

The SS United States, a 53,330-ton liner built in the 1950s to be both unsinkable and the fastest ship to cross the Atlantic, has been deliberately sunk off Florida's Gulf coast. After decades rusting at a pier, it now rests on the seabed as the world's largest artificial reef. Over the coming years, corals, sponges and fish will move in — and so will divers.

Why it's cool: a record-breaking machine gets a second, much slower life as a home for sea creatures.

Modified $450 3D printer builds living materials with more than 100 layers

Phys.org

Researchers at the University of Rochester and Cornell swapped the plastic nozzle of an ordinary hobby 3D printer for a syringe pump that lays down "bio-ink" — living cells mixed into a gel. The result prints living structures over 100 layers tall, with hollow channels, and keeps microbes alive for a month or more. Commercial bioprinters can cost over $100,000; this one costs about $450, and the build instructions are free online.

Why it's cool: living materials that clean water or eat plastic just became something a school lab could try.

How Sky Glabush Conjures Atmospheric Landscapes in Sand and Oil Paint

Colossal

Canadian painter Sky Glabush mixes oil paint with sand — sometimes crushed quartz or raw pigment — to build landscapes that feel more like memories of a place than pictures of it. He works with just one or two color families, starting from a grid or radial structure and then layering and wiping away thin paint. A new solo show opens in London on November 20.

Why it's cool: you can almost feel the texture of the hillsides through the screen.

Centenas de gravuras rupestres com milhares de anos são encontradas em caverna turística na Espanha

Superinteressante

A Cova de la Font Major, na Catalunha, recebe turistas há décadas — mas só em 2019 alguém percebeu traços quase apagados nas paredes. Um novo estudo catalogou 280 gravuras paleolíticas, incluindo 55 figuras de animais, além de formas geométricas e linhas paralelas. Arte rupestre em tamanha quantidade é raridade no Mediterrâneo.

Por que é legal: uma galeria de arte da Idade da Pedra estava, literalmente, debaixo do nariz de todo mundo.

'There's a Georgia O'Keeffe in the Closet' – Words that Led to Painting's Joyous Rediscovery

Good News Network

In 1963, a young woman got an O'Keeffe landscape as a graduation gift; years later she tucked it into a closet among her fur coats because a bridge partner kept staring at it. After she died, her niece remembered an offhand remark — "there's a Georgia O'Keeffe in the closet" — and found the 1930 oil, Near Abiquiu, New Mexico I, still in its original gallery box. Authenticated with help from a 1931 Stieglitz photograph, it goes to auction this week.

Why it's cool: a masterpiece spent decades hiding in plain sight, kept safe by sheer forgetfulness.

Wikipédia

Encomendado por uma fabricante japonesa de calculadoras, a Busicom, o 4004 foi o primeiro microprocessador comercial num único chip: cerca de 2.300 transistores, menor que uma unha. Foi anunciado ao público em meados de novembro de 1971 — e é o tataravô do processador do celular em que você talvez esteja lendo isto.
