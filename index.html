🔬 Ciência & Espaço

Meet the “hell chicken”, the first dinosaur known to specialize in fruit

Phys.org

Paleontologists led by Gregory Funston described Infernogallus jamesi, a small oviraptorosaur from the famous Hell Creek Formation in the US, known from a nearly complete skull. Its jaws and chewing muscles were built for mashing soft fruit — the first dinosaur shown to be adapted to a fruit diet, even though its lineage descended from meat-eaters. The name means “hell chicken”, a nod to where it was found.

Why it’s cool: 67 million years ago, someone was already going for the berries.

The asteroid killed the dinosaurs. Why did crocodiles survive?

ScienceDaily

A UCL-led team combined more than 7,000 fossil records from 400+ crocodile-line species with 245 million years of climate and geography models. Crocodiles have always “followed the warmth”, spreading in hot eras and retreating to the tropics when it cooled. After the impact, equatorial regions likely stayed warm enough to act as refuges — and a modest body size, semi-aquatic habits and very low energy needs did the rest.

Ave gigante da Era do Gelo pode nunca ter existido

Superinteressante

Por mais de 150 anos, ossos grandes encontrados em cavernas da França foram atribuídos a um grou gigante extinto, o Grus primigenia. Agora, a análise de DNA antigo de 18 ossos espalhados pela Europa mostrou que eles eram, na verdade, do bom e velho grou-comum — só que em versões maiores. A diferença de tamanho pode ter a ver com machos e fêmeas, e com como os grous da Era do Gelo eram mais robustos que os de hoje.

Por que é legal: às vezes a ciência avança “desdescobrindo” uma espécie.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Scientists discover the hidden “muscles” trees use to straighten themselves

ScienceDaily

French researchers at INRAE put young poplars on a slowly rotating platform inside an evenly lit sphere, erasing every gravity and light cue. Even so, bent stems gradually straightened over a few weeks — meaning trees can sense their own shape. The trick is “tension wood”: the tree stops making it on one side and starts on the other, so two opposing layers pull like a pair of muscles until the trunk is upright again.

Why it’s cool: trees have something like proprioception.

A new home for orangutans rescued from Borneo’s fires

Phys.org

Nine orangutans rescued from wildfires in West Kalimantan — including four mothers with their young, like Mama Ocha and baby Ocha — were released into Batu Nyambu, a 23,000-hectare protected forest. The YIARI team spent four to eight weeks treating them for burns and malnutrition first. At the final checkup came a bonus: three of the mothers were pregnant again.

Nepal tries invisible “scent fences” to keep big cats away from villages

Mongabay

Big cats mark territory with scent, so researchers are testing lab-made copies of those signals as “BioBoundaries” — an idea that began in Botswana, where one leopard compound noticeably reduced trouble with livestock. In Nepal’s Khata Corridor, Cornell, WWF and local foresters are testing ten compounds; some tigers and leopards sniffed and turned back. Results are still mixed, and the team has narrowed it down to six scents for the next phase.

Why it’s cool: coexistence written in a language the cats already speak.

A new Texas school is named after the janitor everyone calls “Miss Marilyn”

Good News Network

For 39 years Marilyn Hampton kept the schools of Aubrey, Texas clean — and quietly bought supplies, tutored struggling kids and ran the football ticket booth. At a board meeting, the district rolled out a red carpet stitched with “Marilyn Hampton Elementary School”. Now 76 and still helping with toddlers at the district’s early learning center, she said it was an honor she never thought she’d see in her lifetime.

🛠️ Tecnologia, História & Curiosidades

A 2,800-year-old bone flute is the first instrument ever found at Troy

Smithsonian Magazine

After more than 150 years of digging at Troy, archaeologists have finally found a musical instrument: a small flute carved from bone. It dates to roughly the era when the Iliad and the Odyssey were taking shape. It is a rare glimpse of the sound of everyday life in a city we mostly know through stories of heroes.

Why it’s cool: somebody in Troy was playing tunes while Homer was still around.

A dog left a paw print on this mud brick 4,000 years ago

Smithsonian Magazine

At Tavşanlı Höyük in western Turkey, archaeologists sifting through the rubble of a Bronze Age house found a mud brick with a dog’s paw pressed into it — the brick was still soft, drying in the sun, when the dog walked by. It’s a tiny, very relatable moment, and a reminder of how long dogs have been part of human households in the region.

The Tai Hang Fire Dragon Dance, Hong Kong

Atlas Obscura

Every year, a long dragon woven from rattan and straw is studded with thousands of burning incense sticks and paraded through the narrow streets of the old Tai Hang village, in the middle of Hong Kong. Performers twist it through the crowds in a cloud of smoke and glow. At the end of the festivities, the dragon is carried to the water and sent into Victoria Harbour.

Why it’s cool: a village tradition that survived inside one of the densest cities on Earth.

17th-Century Embroidery Meets Hand-Carved Wooden Vessels by Aspen Golann

Colossal

Furniture maker Aspen Golann translates the swirling motifs of 17th-century needlework into solid wood, mixing hand-carving with early American techniques like those of Boston-area Windsor chairs. Her “Crewel Cabinet”, made during a residency at the Metropolitan Museum, is built from 37 staves with 14 colored layers each. It goes on view at Superhouse in New York from November 6.

Escape into Gretchen Scherer’s Maximalist Paintings of Palatial, Art-Filled Spaces

Colossal

Gretchen Scherer paints libraries, studios, museums and grand interiors with walls packed salon-style with framed art — half real, half imagined, and full of playful whimsy. She works on panel, echoing the old paintings she depicts, and leaves out people entirely, so each room feels like someone just stepped out. Her solo show “In Time” runs at Monya Rowe Gallery in New York through January 9.

Manga treasures are on view in New York

Smithsonian Magazine

The Brooklyn Museum’s “Art of Manga” brings together hundreds of original drawings by the artists who shaped Japanese comics, many of them rarely shown in the US. Seeing the hand-inked pages up close — corrections, screentone and all — reveals the craft behind a medium usually consumed in cheap paperback form.

📖 Leitura longa

A New Map of “Empty” Space Challenges a Tranquil Picture of the Galaxy

Quanta Magazine · Perri Thaler

The space between stars isn’t empty — it’s full of thin gas, and textbooks say that gas settles into neat cold and warm phases. Two Technion astronomers combined a 3D dust map of our galactic neighborhood with a map of the ultraviolet light heating it, and found that about 41% of the gas sits in an “unstable” in-between state, wrapped around cold clouds. If it holds up, it paints the galaxy as constantly churning — and might help explain why galaxies are so oddly slow at making stars.

Perfect for a slow Sunday: a calm piece about how restless the cosmos actually is.
