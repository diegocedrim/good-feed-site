Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System

ScienceDaily

A team led by Kevin Hoy used ESO's Very Large Telescope to watch a brown dwarf called CD-35 2722 wobble slightly back and forth. The tug comes from a companion at least as massive as Jupiter, which would make it an "exosatellite": a moon orbiting an object that's halfway between a planet and a star.

If it holds up, it would be the first plausible moon detected outside our Solar System. It also blurs the line between what counts as a moon and what counts as a planet.

A "moon" the size of Jupiter, around a "star" that never quite made it.

New evidence suggests Spinosaurus spent most of its life underwater

ScienceDaily

Yale researchers led by Caleb Gordon trained machine-learning models on modern animals, using more than 11,000 bone measurements, and then applied them to fossils. Their verdict is that Spinosaurus spent most of its time submerged, something closer to a giant crocodile than a riverbank wader.

The same method surprised them in the other direction too. Mesosaurs, small ancient reptiles long thought to be fully aquatic, seem to have moved between water and land the way alligators do today.

This 150-million-year-old bird had dinosaur teeth and a strange secret

ScienceDaily

Paleontologists from the Chinese Academy of Sciences described Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis, a crow-sized bird found in Fujian Province. It had serrated teeth and a sickle-shaped claw like a small raptor dinosaur.

The surprise is that its body was built for life on the ground. That makes it the oldest known case of a bird lineage giving up flight, only a few million years after birds first took to the air.

Birds had barely learned to fly when some of them were already deciding it wasn't worth it.

World Snow Leopard Day: Community action and new science foster coexistence with humans

Mongabay

Nepal's latest survey counted about 397 snow leopards. In the Lapchi Valley, researchers found them sharing territory with common leopards and Himalayan wolves without much trouble, because each predator mostly hunts different prey. Wild blue sheep make up nearly half of a snow leopard's diet.

Conservationists say the key to peace with herders is practical support, like predator-proof corrals and compensation for lost livestock. In Kyrgyzstan, a new 800,000-hectare corridor gives about 300 snow leopards room to roam alongside traditional herding.

Water, carbon & the 'tree of life': Interview with Venezuela's guardian of morichales

Mongabay

Morichales are swampy forests of moriche palm (Mauritia flexuosa) that grow around springs and slow streams across South America. They work like giant sponges, soaking up water in the rainy season and releasing it slowly through droughts. They can also store as much as 590 tonnes of carbon per hectare.

Venezuelan ecologist Miguel Ángel Sánchez-Mercado combines fieldwork with work alongside local and Indigenous communities, arguing that a wetland only survives if the people around it value it.

The moriche palm is our own buriti, the same tree behind the veredas of the Brazilian Cerrado.

The farmer growing a future under Ghana's trees

Positive News

Susana Afrifa Ankrah grows cocoa in Ghana's Atewa Range, and she now does it in the shade of native trees instead of on cleared land. Through a community forest programme, farmers like her get training, tools and payments for keeping the forest standing.

The result goes against intuition: participants report yields around 80% higher, along with healthier soil and farms that cope better with heat and dry spells.

Chocolate that helps protect a forest instead of replacing it.

This mysterious mushroom makes people see hundreds of tiny humans

ScienceDaily

For decades, people in southwest China and the northern Philippines have described the same odd vision after eating a certain wild mushroom: crowds of tiny people walking around the room. University of Utah mycologists Colin Domnauer and Bryn Dentinger DNA-sequenced 53 specimens and identified the culprit as Lanmaoa asiatica.

The puzzling part is that it contains no psilocybin or any other known psychoactive compound, so it's probably making a molecule nobody has described yet. Along the way, the team also found four new fungal species.

Many different people describe the same hallucination, and science still hasn't found the molecule behind it.

Southern Africa's first known stenciled rock art found hiding in plain sight

Phys.org

At the La Rochelle rock shelter in South Africa's Mpumalanga province, researchers David Pearce and Kiah Johnson noticed that two little red human figures, just 9 cm tall, weren't painted by hand. Someone made a custom template, maybe from beeswax, clay or wood, held it against the rock and sprayed or splattered pigment around it.

Purpose-made stencils like this are rare anywhere in the world. They had been sitting among thousands of ordinary paintings for decades. Nobody knows yet who made them, or when.

Colossal

Como histórias e o imaginário cultural transformam hotéis em pontos turísticos

Jornal da USP

Por que tem gente que visita um hotel sem se hospedar nele? Fernando Silvestre parte de teóricos do turismo, como Dean MacCannell, para mostrar que um lugar não nasce atração: ele vira atração com as histórias, personagens e imagens que circulam sobre ele.

Os exemplos são o Copacabana Palace, com o glamour de hóspedes como Marilyn Monroe, e o IceHotel sueco, que é reconstruído todo inverno com gelo do rio Torne e quartos esculpidos por artistas.

Um hotel que derrete e é refeito a cada ano, como uma obra de arte que muda sempre.

25 de outubro de 1671: Cassini descobre Jápeto, a lua de duas caras

Wikipédia

Em outubro de 1671, Giovanni Domenico Cassini achou uma nova lua de Saturno, Jápeto. Ele percebeu que a lua aparecia bem quando estava de um lado do planeta e quase sumia do outro, e concluiu, com razão, que um hemisfério é claro e o outro é escuro.

Três séculos depois, as sondas confirmaram: um lado escuro como carvão, o outro claro como neve, e uma cordilheira ao longo do equador com picos de mais de 20 km, entre os mais altos do Sistema Solar.

The ladder of knowledge

Aeon · Alexander V Gheorghiu · ~20 min

Hilbert dreamed that every mathematical truth could be derived from a fixed set of axioms, and then Gödel showed that it can't. UCL logician Alexander Gheorghiu tells that story and then reads it in a hopeful way.

Drawing on the philosopher Michael Dummett, he argues that truth isn't a ceiling we keep failing to reach. It's more like a ladder that we build as we climb, where each rung we add lets us reach a bit higher.

A gentle introduction to Gödel for a slow Sunday.
