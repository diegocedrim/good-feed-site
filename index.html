🔬 Ciência & Espaço

The asteroid that killed the dinosaurs may have helped ants conquer the world

ScienceDaily

A team led by Lukas Schrader at the University of Münster compared the genomes of 163 ant species and found something odd: right after the dinosaur-ending impact, about 66 million years ago, several ant lineages had independent bursts of "jumping genes", DNA segments that copy and paste themselves around the genome.

Those bursts seem to have expanded the families of odor-receptor genes that ants use to read chemical signals for navigation and recognizing nestmates. The lineages with the most jumping genes also tend to have the most species today.

DNA once written off as "genomic parasites" may have given ants their sense of smell.

Against all expectations, researchers control the magnetic core of a single atom

Phys.org

Physicists at Delft University of Technology managed to steer the nuclear spin of one single atom using radio waves. Most researchers thought you couldn't manipulate the nucleus that directly, since it is so well shielded and so weakly magnetic.

Being able to write and read information in the nucleus of an atom could become useful for storing quantum information, which needs exactly that kind of quiet, protected place.

The tiniest possible hard drive just got a little more real.

Earwax is both slippery and sticky, and this weird combo might lead to safer insect repellents

Phys.org

Engineer David Hu and colleagues watched ants try to climb over earwax. On smooth wax, the ants slipped and fell: the porous, sponge-like wax sucked the moisture out of their sticky feet, and they spent ages grooming themselves afterward.

A synthetic version that copied only the wax's physical structure, not its chemistry, stopped ants too. Earwax can also soak up about twice its weight in water and still stay put, which may help protect our eardrums.

Your ears have been running a bug-defense system all along.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Scientists discover a secret glow in toxic fire salamanders

ScienceDaily

Shine ultraviolet light on a European fire salamander and it glows green and cyan, mostly from its skin glands and the toxic secretions they release. The team, from the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology and Barcelona's natural science museum, found that the secretions keep glowing for more than a day after leaving the animal.

Nobody knows yet which molecule makes the light, or what it's for. It might help salamanders spot each other, or make their black-and-yellow warning even louder to predators.

A familiar forest animal turns out to have a hidden neon layer.

Ben Franklin wasn't serious when he called the wild turkey a "respectable" symbol, but he wasn't wrong either

Smithsonian Magazine

Wild turkeys lived in North America for hundreds of thousands of years before overhunting pushed them close to disappearing. Today they're back across much of the continent.

This piece tells the story of that comeback: a tough, adaptable, stubborn bird, with some help from people who decided it was worth saving.

A conservation success story you can see walking across the road.

Kew launches a global seed platform to unblock nature restoration

Phys.org

Many restoration projects get stuck on something simple: they can't find seeds of the right native plants. The Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, has launched SeedPOD, a free, open platform for finding native seed collections around the world.

It also shares the practical know-how for actually growing those plants, which is often the hardest part.

Rewilding starts with a very humble thing: the right seed in the right place.

Líquido que interrompe a cárie sem perfuração no dente passa na fase 3 de estudos nos EUA

Só Notícia Boa

Um estudo de fase 3 da Universidade de Michigan testou o diamino fluoreto de prata em crianças pequenas: aplicado sem broca e sem anestesia, o líquido paralisou mais da metade das cáries tratadas.

Divers recover a mysterious cauldron that hid at the bottom of an English river for centuries

Smithsonian Magazine

Divers brought up a three-legged iron cauldron from the River Wear in northern England, where it had been lying for hundreds of years.

How it got there is still an open question: dropped, lost, or thrown in on purpose. Now the object waits for historians to piece together its story.

A little river mystery, the kind you can mull over with your coffee.

Specialists removed three century-old murals from a French monastery. Beneath, they made "a discovery for the history of art"

Smithsonian Magazine

Odilon Redon's murals Day, Night and Silence decorate the library of Fontfroide Abbey, an 11th-century monastery near Narbonne. When conservators took them down for the first time, to lend them to an exhibition in Paris, they found smaller and more colorful sketches Redon had drawn straight onto the wall.

The abbey's director is "99 percent sure" Redon made them during a ten-day visit in 1910, probably to test how the murals would look in the room. The sketches will be visible until March, and then covered again.

A painter's private rehearsal, hidden for more than a century.

A macro journey underwater reveals the radiant geometries of coral

Colossal

Filmmaker Sandro Bocci, of the Julia Set Lab collective, pointed extreme macro lenses at coral reefs for a short film. Up close, the corals turn into glowing patterns, textures and repeating shapes that are impossible to see with the naked eye.

It's a few minutes of pure color and rhythm, almost like watching living fractals breathe.

Best watched full-screen, in silence, with the last sip of coffee.

How astronomers and art historians are working together to identify the inspiration behind famous works of art

Smithsonian Magazine · edição de novembro

When a painting or a poem shows the sky, which sky was it? Scholars dig into famous works to recover the stories behind them, and sometimes that means working out which Moon, planet or star is hiding in a hazy, dusty patch of canvas.

This magazine feature follows the unusual partnership between astronomers and art historians, who team up to read the night skies that artists actually saw.

Art history and astronomy in the same frame. A lovely one for a slow evening.
