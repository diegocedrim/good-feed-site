O Universo poderia ter 26,7 bilhões de anos (e nenhuma matéria ou energia escuras)

Superinteressante

Todo livro diz que o cosmos tem 13,8 bilhões de anos e que 95% dele é feito de matéria e energia escuras — coisas que ninguém nunca detectou diretamente, só deduziu porque as contas não fecham sem elas. A Super explica um modelo alternativo que dispensa as duas e, de quebra, praticamente dobra a idade do Universo.

Por que é legal: é um lembrete de que até os “números decorados” da ciência continuam abertos a perguntas.

NASA smashed into an asteroid. Now Hera has revealed a strange new world

ScienceDaily

Europe's Hera spacecraft sent back its first close-up of Didymos, a 780-metre asteroid, revealing boulders, craters and odd groove-like patterns never seen in such detail. Its little moon Dimorphos — the one NASA's DART probe deliberately hit in 2022, shortening its orbit by about 33 minutes — still sits mostly in shadow. Scientists now wonder whether debris from that impact drifted across and marked the bigger rock; sharper images are due on 25 November.

Why it's cool: this is humanity learning, carefully, how to nudge an asteroid out of the way.

An ancient virus may help explain what makes the human brain different

ScienceDaily

Researchers at Lund University found that a scrap of viral DNA, left in the chimpanzee genome by an infection millions of years ago, silences a gene called LINC00662 in chimp brain organoids — while the same gene stays switched on in humans. When they snipped the viral sequence out with CRISPR, the gene woke up again in chimp cells; turning it off in human organoids dampened genes tied to building neurons and their connections.

Why it's cool: part of what makes our brains ours may come down to an old virus that infected our cousins, not us.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

A 'lost' firefly species flashes back into view in the US

Mongabay

On a June night in a Delaware meadow, entomologist Christopher Heckscher noticed amber flashes in a rhythm he didn't recognise. It was the similar firefly, Pyractomena similis, unrecorded anywhere since 1983 and never before seen in that state. It's one of 28 North American fireflies considered “lost to science” — and researchers suspect many are simply waiting for someone to look closely.

Hidden ecosystem: Scientists discover 245 animals living inside a single deep-sea rock

ScienceDaily

Scientists at Bremen's MARUM centre cracked open 244 fist-sized metal nodules from the deep Pacific floor and found 4,139 tiny animals living in their nooks — 17 per rock on average, and one record-holder housing 245. These communities differ from those in the surrounding mud, making each nodule a little apartment block that took millions of years to form.

Why it's cool: even a "rock" four kilometres down turns out to be somebody's home.

Essas algas marinhas se reproduzem sozinhas quando não encontram parceiros próximos

Superinteressante

A kelp, alga parda que forma verdadeiras florestas submarinas, consegue se reproduzir tanto com parceiro quanto sozinha. Experimentos com a espécie Laminaria pallida, da costa da África do Sul e da Namíbia, mostraram que ela só apela para a reprodução solo quando não há ninguém por perto — e que esses descendentes crescem mais devagar.

Por que é legal: um plano B evolutivo para não deixar a floresta sumir.

"It has brought tears of pure joy": the project donating bicycles to refugees

Positive News

Since 2013, The Bike Project has taken unwanted bicycles, fixed them up in workshops in London and Birmingham, and handed them to refugees — more than 16,000 so far, across almost 40 UK cities. For people with little money and long distances to cover, a bike means getting to class, to appointments, to friends. One recipient describes it simply as a second chance at life.

Why it's cool: an estimated 38 million bikes sit idle in British sheds — a very simple fix hiding in plain sight.

Soberania genética na arqueologia inspirou criação do primeiro laboratório de DNA antigo do Brasil

Jornal da USP

O Museu de Arqueologia e Etnologia da USP ganhou o primeiro laboratório do país inteiramente dedicado à arqueogenética, coordenado por André Strauss e Tábita Hünemeier. A ideia é que ossos e amostras brasileiras sejam estudados aqui, sem precisar viajar para laboratórios estrangeiros. O primeiro projeto analisa 63 pessoas enterradas na antiga igreja de Mazagão Velho, no Amapá — vila fundada em 1770 por famílias vindas de Marrocos — para descobrir se africanos também estavam entre elas.

When the Saudi desert was green: A 125,000-year-old lakeside reveals how early humans lived

Phys.org

Today the Empty Quarter is one of the driest places on Earth, but 125,000 years ago it held Lake Mundafan. On its ancient shore, archaeologists found bones of hippos, long-horned buffalo, camels, oryx and ostriches alongside 5,511 stone tools, mostly flint. The finds suggest people there hunted, worked stone and processed plants — possibly even using toxic plant compounds on their weapons.

Why it's cool: hippos in Arabia is a mental image worth having with your coffee.

Qual a diferença entre bilhar, sinuca e snooker?

Superinteressante

Bilhar é o nome da família inteira; ele nasceu quando um jogo francês de gramado, o croqué, foi levado para mesas dentro dos palácios no inverno. O snooker veio em 1875, criado por um oficial inglês. E a sinuca brasileira, com uma única bola vermelha, teria surgido em salões do começo do século 20, quando jogadores encurtaram as partidas para disputar mais rodadas valendo cerveja.

Smithsonian Magazine

Indiana Jones at the fish market: David Ebert on a life spent searching for 'lost sharks'

Mongabay

Known as the “Lost Shark Guy,” marine biologist David Ebert has spent four decades hunting not for famous predators but for the obscure ones: rare sharks, rays and chimaeras, often found on fish-market tables rather than out at sea. In one story, a Sri Lankan fisherman looked at a photo on a phone and brought back a species new to science the very next day. Ebert has described 56 new species, tracked down a guitarfish not recorded since 1926, and trained local fishers and researchers along the way. At 68, he has no plans to slow down.

Why it's cool: a gentle, curious life story — and proof that discoveries still happen with a phone, a market and a lot of patience.
