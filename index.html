Webb catches a supernova in the early universe just days after it exploded

Phys.org

The NEXUS program points the James Webb telescope at the same patch of sky every two months, looking for anything that changed. Between two visits 71 days apart, one tiny spot got more than three times brighter. It's a probable supernova, now called AT 2026abck, in a galaxy we see as it was when the universe was only about 1.2 billion years old.

Modelling suggests the star had exploded only 4 to 10 days (in its own time) before Webb saw it. That makes it the first supernova this far away caught so early without help from gravitational lensing.

A star's last moments, seen 12 billion years later, almost as they happened.

Octopus intelligence may come from an ancient reshuffling of their DNA

ScienceDaily

Researchers at the University of Vienna looked at how the genomes of octopuses, squid and cuttlefish are folded in three dimensions inside the cell. Hundreds of millions of years ago, big chunks of cephalopod DNA were rearranged, putting regions that used to be far apart right next to each other.

The large folded "domains" barely changed over evolution. The smaller "loops", though, vary a lot between species and tissues, and many of them sit near genes that build the nervous system. The idea is that new physical contacts in the DNA let new gene networks form without breaking the old ones.

It's not only which genes you have, but how the DNA is folded up.

A tiny Jurassic swimmer and the soft palate that made our brains possible

ScienceDaily

A 165-million-year-old fossil from Inner Mongolia has been named Megacauda sungei: a half-metre, two-kilo mammal relative with backward-curving teeth for catching slippery prey. It's the largest known mammaliaform from the Jurassic and the earliest evidence of a mammal ancestor living in water.

The surprise is in its throat. It had signs of a soft palate, the tissue that lets you breathe and swallow at the same time. That's what allows a baby to nurse on milk, and the energy-rich milk diet is a big part of how mammals could grow large brains.

Chinook salmon rush back into the Klamath River after four dams come down

Mongabay

In 2024, four dams were removed from the Klamath River, which runs through southern Oregon and northern California. They had blocked the salmon for about a century. Researchers used sonar that can "see" through murky water and counted 7,742 Chinook heading upstream that first autumn. In 2025 the count passed 13,000.

The fish have already moved back into 345 km of river they couldn't reach before, covering 88% of their known historical spawning grounds. The scientists say they were surprised by how fast and how widely the salmon responded.

Open the door and the river knows the way home.

Shedding light on the sea: a talk with Thai photographer Sirachai Arunrugstichai

Mongabay

"Shin" trained in biomedical science, fell in love with the reefs while diving in Thailand, and became a marine biologist and photojournalist. His best-known picture shows Marium, an orphaned baby dugong, resting against her caretaker. The photo travelled the world and helped build support for a marine protected area in Trang province.

His camera has even led to real science. Strange squid behaviour he photographed in Myanmar's Mergui Archipelago turned into a peer-reviewed study on how squid choose their mates. He's realistic that one photo can't fix the ocean on its own, but he's hopeful about Thai seas now that fishing rules are better enforced.

Photos: Taiwan's old tea terraces come back to life

Mongabay

Shenkeng, a district 10 km from Taipei, used to be tea country. In 1897 tea made up 22% of Taiwan's exports, and the local Wenshan pouchong became famous. Then the city grew, and forest swallowed the stone terraces. Now farming families and researchers are rebuilding them by hand.

They're also updating them for a wetter, stormier climate: drip irrigation with soil sensors, mulch made from fallen leaves, hardier tea varieties and companion trees that hold the slopes. A tea bush can take 5 to 30 years to reach its best, so this is work for the next generation.

Vovô da roça leva marmita para a neta médica no trabalho

Só Notícia Boa

Seu Nezinho mora no interior da Bahia e passou o dia na roça. Mesmo morto de cansaço, foi até a clínica da neta Waneska, que é médica, levar um almoço caseiro. Quando perguntaram por quê, a resposta foi simples: porque ama a neta.

Waneska é a primeira neta dele, e os dois são grudados desde que ela era pequena. Seu Nezinho nunca foi à escola: aprendeu sozinho a ler, a escrever e a fazer contas de cabeça mais rápido que muita gente. A neta o chama de "uma mente brilhante que não teve oportunidade de brilhar".

The students who built a home for classmates with nowhere to live

Reasons to be Cheerful

At UC Davis, undergraduates run Aggie House, a set of low-cost apartments for fellow students who have lost their housing. Rent is $200–300 a month, about 90% below the local average. It goes up $50 each month to help residents move on within a year. Student volunteers come in from 7 to 11 p.m. to cook dinner and keep each other company.

It's part of a wider movement. In Boston, Y2Y was started by two Harvard students in a church basement and offers short stays to anyone aged 18–24. In both places the people helping are close in age to the people being helped, and residents say that's what makes it feel safe.

Berlin's app that tells you when your neighbourhood tree is thirsty

Good News Network

"Gieß den Kiez" means "water your neighbourhood". The app maps about 900,000 street trees in Berlin. Trees that need water show up as orange or red dots, along with the nearest public water pumps. You can adopt a tree, and the app tells you how much to give it based on species, age and the weather.

CityLAB Berlin launched it in 2020, and thousands of people now use it during summer heatwaves. The code is open source, so Potsdam, Leipzig, Magdeburg and a district of Paris have made their own versions.

Users say it feels almost like a video game, except the tree is real.

Hooked on tuna: people have been catching it for 44,000 years

Phys.org

The oldest evidence comes from Jerimalai Cave in East Timor, where 44,000-year-old tuna bones were found. Deep channels there brought the fish close to shore. Around 9,000 to 11,000 years ago, people in a Greek cave above Kiladha Bay left thousands of tuna bones behind.

On the Pacific coast of North America, the Nuu-chah-nulth fished bluefin for more than 5,000 years. At night they went out in canoes lit by fire and followed the glow of bioluminescent plankton to find the fish.

Your can of tuna has a family tree older than cave painting in Europe.

Kanmon Pedestrian Tunnel: walking under the sea between two Japanese islands

Atlas Obscura

In Shimonoseki, an elevator takes you down to a 780-metre tunnel that runs under the Kanmon Strait, linking Honshu, Japan's main island, to Kyushu. Joggers and curious tourists walk it every day.

Halfway through, a line painted on the floor marks the border between Yamaguchi and Fukuoka prefectures. Visitors like to stand with one foot on each side. Above them, ships pass through one of the busiest straits in Japan.

An Impact Structure With Legs: Australia's "Spider Crater" from space

NASA Earth Observatory

In Western Australia's Kimberley region, Landsat 9 photographed a spider-shaped scar about 4 km across, at the centre of an impact structure measuring 13 by 11 km. A meteorite hit here between 600 and 900 million years ago.

The "legs" are ridges of hard sandstone. They were pushed up when the crater floor rebounded after the impact, and they stayed standing while the softer rock around them wore away. For years geologists argued it might be volcanic. The question was settled by shatter cones, fan-shaped fractures that only form under an extreme shock.

The "in awe" artist painting a wilder Britain

Positive News

Rebecca Howard spent a year visiting and painting nine rewilding projects across the UK. She painted restored saltmarsh in Lancashire, a fragment of temperate rainforest in Cornwall, Yorkshire wildflower meadows, beaver territory in Devon and turtle-dove habitat in Somerset.

Her show "A Year in the Wild" is on at Knepp, in Sussex, until 18 October, and sales of cards and prints support Rewilding Britain. She says she wanted to paint "the beauty of life coming from decay", like the porcelain mushrooms she placed on a felled larch.

13 de outubro de 1892: o primeiro cometa descoberto por fotografia

Wikipédia

Nessa noite, o astrônomo americano Edward Emerson Barnard encontrou um cometa numa chapa fotográfica, e não olhando pela ocular. Foi a primeira vez que isso aconteceu. A fotografia combinava com ele: aos nove anos, sem dinheiro para estudar, Barnard foi trabalhar num estúdio fotográfico em Nashville. Depois construiu o próprio telescópio e aprendeu astronomia sozinho.

Ele ainda faria um grande catálogo fotográfico de nebulosas escuras e, em 1916, encontraria a "Estrela de Barnard", uma das estrelas mais próximas do Sol.

Deskilling the body

Aeon · Melanie Challenger

Challenger argues that intelligence doesn't live only in the head. It grows out of the body moving, sensing and finding its way through the world. So every time a device does that work for us, we practise it a little less. Her examples include GPS and spatial memory, AI drafts and the confidence to write, and how fast muscles fade when we stop using them.

The essay doesn't preach doom. Her point is that the body learns back quickly: walk, get lost a little, and use tools to revise your own effort rather than replace it. About 24 minutes of reading. It goes well with a coffee and, afterwards, a walk without your phone.
