🔬 Ciência & Espaço

All 5 genetic "letters" of DNA and RNA discovered in a 4.5-billion-year-old asteroid

ScienceDaily

Japanese scientists analysing the 5.4 grams of asteroid Ryugu brought home by the Hayabusa2 probe have found all five nucleobases — the molecular letters that spell out DNA and RNA. The samples were handled in ultra-clean conditions to rule out contamination from Earth. NASA's Bennu samples showed the same thing last year, which suggests these building blocks were scattered all over the young Solar System.

Why it's cool: the alphabet of life may have been raining down on Earth long before life began.

A tiny shift exposes a cold brown dwarf masquerading as an early galaxy

Phys.org

Webb spotted an object nicknamed Capotauro that looked like one of the very first galaxies, shining just 100 million years after the Big Bang. But between 2022 and 2026 it moved a tiny bit across the sky, and real distant galaxies don't do that. It turns out to be a cold brown dwarf in our own Milky Way, about 2,400 light-years away and roughly as warm as a hot cup of tea.

Why it's cool: a cosmic impostor caught because it couldn't stay still.

Neptune's elusive Trojan asteroids finally come into view

Phys.org

A team led by Queen's University Belfast used early data from the new Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile to find new long-lived members of Neptune's trailing Trojan cloud, roughly doubling the known population. For a decade these faint rocks were hidden against the bright centre of the Milky Way. Rubin's nine-year survey should turn up dozens to hundreds more.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Expedição submarina encontra 31 espécies novas nas águas do Brasil

Superinteressante

Em abril, 30 cientistas a bordo de um navio do Schmidt Ocean Institute vasculharam o mar entre Salvador e Fortaleza, entre 200 e 1.200 metros de profundidade — a chamada zona crepuscular. Voltaram com 31 espécies desconhecidas: nove águas-vivas, sete sifonóforos, sete ctenóforos com cílios cintilantes e até um delicado “verme-teia-de-fada”. As fotos feitas pelo robô submarino parecem joias de vidro.

O maior ecossistema habitável da Terra ainda guarda surpresas bem aqui na nossa costa.

A 50-year-old mystery of vanishing frogs reveals a hidden killer

ScienceDaily

Between 1979 and 1982, frogs mysteriously crashed at the Boracéia field station in Brazil's Atlantic Forest. By testing more than 370 museum specimens, Smithsonian and Penn State researchers showed the chytrid fungus arrived just then. The happy part: recent surveys found 12 of the 17 species again, apparently now more tolerant of the fungus.

Why it's cool: old museum jars solved a cold case — and the frogs came back.

Venomous Indonesian primate feared extinct still clings on

Mongabay

The Bangka slow loris hadn't appeared in scientific papers since around 1937 and was tagged “possibly extinct.” But a local rescue centre has taken in more than 90 of these big-eyed animals from backyards and plantations across all four districts of the Indonesian island. Researchers now say the tag should go — while warning that mining, palm oil and power lines still threaten it.

Payment scheme in Botswana pays communities to coexist with wildlife

Mongabay

Two tiny villages on the edge of the Okavango Delta put camera traps around their homes, and every elephant, hyena or leopard photographed earns money for a shared community fund. An elephant is worth the most. Villagers decide together how to spend it — a simple way to reward people who live alongside big animals.

Brasileiro de 16 anos conquista 3 medalhas internacionais em Economia, Física e Astronomia

Só Notícia Boa

Arthur Alencar Spuri, estudante de Fortaleza, levou ouro na Olimpíada Internacional de Economia, na China, com a maior pontuação já registrada no torneio — o primeiro sul-americano a liderar a classificação. Ainda ganhou prata em Física, na Colômbia, e em Astronomia, no Vietnã. O segredo? Cerca de cinco horas por dia refazendo provas antigas e estudando os próprios erros.

Um ano inteiro de pódios, e ele ainda está no segundo ano do ensino médio.

This Wheelchair Skateboarding Teacher Already Conquered Mountains and Marathons

Good News Network

A fall from a tree at 19 left Xie Junwu paralysed from the waist down. Since then he has set a Guinness record for the longest wheelchair wheelie, climbed Mount Huangshan four times and finished 32 marathons. Now, in Nanchang, he runs what may be China's first wheelchair skateboarding school — and dreams of hosting the country's first competition.

Using sentimental possessions makes them more meaningful, study finds

Phys.org

Grandpa's watch in a drawer or on your wrist? Across five studies, psychologists found that actually using cherished objects makes them more meaningful, not less. Regular use keeps the memory behind the object present, and that makes it feel more like part of who we are.

Why it's cool: permission to drink from the good china on a Tuesday.

“Osso da sorte”: estudo sobre origem em aves e répteis dá pistas sobre evolução dos dinossauros

Jornal da USP

Aquele ossinho em forma de forquilha que a gente puxa no frango de Natal é a fúrcula. Pesquisadores da USP de Ribeirão Preto acompanharam embriões de aves e répteis e viram que, na inhambu, ela nasce de três peças separadas que depois se fundem. A fusão pode ter começado nos primeiros dinossauros terópodes, muito antes de seus braços virarem asas.

O osso da sorte é, literalmente, uma herança dos dinossauros.

Greece Has Its Own Pompeii, and the Ruins Tell a Captivating Story of a Seafaring Bronze Age Society

Smithsonian Magazine

On Santorini, the town of Akrotiri was buried by ash when the Thera volcano erupted some 3,600 years ago. Under the ash, archaeologists found multi-storey houses, vivid frescoes of ships and swallows, and a remarkably sophisticated seafaring town. This piece walks through what the ruins reveal about the people who lived there.

Is There Anything Left in the Dream of Cold Fusion?

Quanta Magazine

Forget the 1989 lab-bench scandal: there is a real kind of fusion that happens at room temperature. When a muon, the electron's heavy cousin, takes an electron's place, it pulls two hydrogen nuclei close enough to fuse, then drifts off to do it again. It will probably never power your house, but physicists in Japan, Italy, France and at CERN use these shrunken exotic atoms as ultra-precise probes of the laws of nature. It's a lovely story about a dream that failed and turned out useful anyway.
