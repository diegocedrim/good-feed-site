Fósseis brasileiros de 115 milhões de anos podem conter resquícios de sangue de dinossauro

Superinteressante

Pesquisadores da Universidade Regional do Cariri (URCA) e da UFRJ dizem ter encontrado o que parecem ser hemácias fossilizadas num osso da perna de um Aratasaurus museunacionali, um dinossauro de uns três metros que viveu na Chapada do Araripe.

O segredo da preservação: depois de morrer, o animal afundou na água e foi coberto por uma camada de micróbios que selou a carcaça. Sangue é um dos vestígios mais raros da paleontologia — e outros cientistas ainda pedem cautela antes de bater o martelo.

Por que é legal: o nome do bicho homenageia o Museu Nacional, e agora ele pode guardar uma das descobertas mais delicadas da paleontologia.

NASA smashed into an asteroid. Now Hera is closing in to see what happened

ScienceDaily

In 2022, NASA's DART probe hit the small asteroid Dimorphos and shortened its orbit around its bigger partner, Didymos, by about 33 minutes — far more than simulations predicted. Europe's Hera spacecraft has now caught both rocks as separate dots from about 1,280 km away.

Over the coming months Hera will survey the crater, release two shoebox-sized CubeSats for close-ups, and maybe even try landing. The first detailed images of Dimorphos are due on November 25.

Why it's cool: humanity's first planetary-defence test finally gets its crime-scene investigator.

Which infinity is bigger? Under alternative mathematical rules, a 2D map emerges

Phys.org

Since Cantor, mathematicians have pictured infinities lined up on a single ladder, from smaller to bigger. William Chan of TU Wien swapped one foundational rule (the axiom of choice) for another (the axiom of determinacy) and found that infinities then spread out on a two-dimensional map — some simply can't be ranked against each other.

Oddly, the arithmetic of infinity becomes simpler and more orderly in this world. It doesn't settle how many infinities there are, but offers a fresh way to organize them.

Why it's cool: change one rule of the game and "bigger than" stops being a straight line.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Miracle Recovery Sees Seabirds Reclaim Former Colony After Avian Flu Wiped Them Out

Good News Network

In 2022 bird flu emptied the seabird colony at Pagham Harbour, on England's south coast. This year it counted 756 sandwich terns — up from zero — plus more little terns, ringed plovers and oystercatchers.

The RSPB and volunteers put up predator-proof fencing, watched for rats, cleared vegetation for nesting and set up dog-walking areas away from the birds.

Why it's cool: proof that, given a little room, wildlife can bounce back fast.

It's Not Just You: Some Fast-Flying Birds Also Get Jet Lag

Smithsonian Magazine

Manx shearwaters cover huge distances so quickly that their internal clocks fall out of step with local time. Since these seabirds use the Sun's position as a compass — which only works if you know what time it is — the lag nudges their navigation slightly off course.

Why it's cool: even born navigators get groggy after a long-haul flight.

Sapos e lagartos têm febre? Variação térmica ambiental favorece comportamento de defesa

Jornal da USP

Anfíbios e répteis não produzem calor próprio, mas quando ficam doentes procuram lugares mais quentes para "fazer febre" — a chamada febre comportamental. Uma metanálise da USP e da Unesp, publicada na Proceedings of the Royal Society B, mostrou que essa resposta é mais forte em espécies que vivem onde a temperatura varia muito ao longo do dia.

O efeito apareceu mais em anfíbios do que em répteis, e foi maior contra bactérias do que contra vírus ou fungos.

Por que é legal: um sapo doente que vai tomar sol está, no fundo, tomando o seu antitérmico ao contrário.

Só Notícia Boa

Em Foz do Iguaçu (PR), um pai almoçava segurando o filho adormecido no colo quando um garçom do Jardim da Cerveja apareceu com um bercinho de rodinhas e uma coberta. O bebê seguiu dormindo ao lado da mesa e o pai, enfim, comeu com as duas mãos.

Bodyweight exercises combat frailty in older adults

Jornal da USP · USP News

At a public health clinic (UBS) in Sorocaba, USP researchers had older adults — average age 74 — do 30-minute bodyweight sessions three times a week: squats, core work, 30 seconds on, 30 seconds off. No gym, no equipment.

After 24 weeks, every participant who started out frail had improved, and about 69% of the "pre-frail" ones became fully robust. Leg strength, walking speed and blood pressure all got better.

Why it's cool: a cheap recipe the SUS could copy anywhere — and a nudge to go do some squats.

Who needs a golden ticket? The race to future-proof chocolate

Positive News

Master's student Maha Khan made six tiny batches of chocolate — "The Reading 100" — from cocoa grown in greenhouses at the University of Reading, in England. It's the home of the International Cocoa Quarantine Centre, which keeps more than 300 cocoa varieties disease-free for two years before sending them to breeders.

With heat, disease and ageing trees squeezing harvests in West Africa, that living library matters — and Khan hopes her bars help it find new funding.

Why it's cool: the future of chocolate is being quietly safeguarded in a greenhouse in rainy England.

Faded Over Millennia, the Intricately Carved Sandstone Walls of This Ancient Egyptian Shrine Will See Color Once Again

Smithsonian Magazine

The Shrine of Taharqa, a 2,700-year-old sandstone chapel now inside Oxford's Ashmolean Museum, was once brightly painted — but time washed it back to bare stone. Instead of touching the carvings, the museum will use digital projections to lay the original colours back onto the walls.

Why it's cool: a way to see the ancient world as its builders did, without a single brushstroke on the real thing.

Ultrasound reveals hidden tongue movements behind centuries-old language change

Phys.org

In Blackburn, Lancashire, many older people still pronounce the old "strong R" in words like star and heart — a sound most of England dropped long ago. Lancaster University linguists used the same ultrasound used in pregnancy scans to watch speakers' tongues from below.

Older speakers made a firm R gesture; younger ones made a weaker one, even when it was hard to hear. A sound change caught in progress, inside the mouth.

Why it's cool: you can literally watch an accent fade, one generation at a time.

Egiategia in Ciboure, France

Atlas Obscura

On France's Basque coast, the Egiategia winery does something unusual: it ferments its wine in vats anchored to the seafloor. The steady cold, darkness and gentle swell of the ocean replace the classic cellar.

Why it's cool: a wine whose cellar is the Atlantic itself.

🎨 Arte & Design

Great Art Explained Traces the Incredible Story of Henri Matisse's 'The Red Studio'

Colossal

Matisse painted The Red Studio in 1911: his workshop drowned in a single red, with almost no perspective, so that colour itself builds the room. Critics hated it, it didn't sell at the 1913 Armory Show, and the artist tucked it away for about a decade.

It later hung in a London nightclub before ending up at MoMA as a landmark of modern art. Art historian James Payne tells the whole story in a short video.

Why it's cool: a reminder that "revolutionary" and "ugly" are sometimes the same painting, decades apart.

Visible Brushstrokes Push on a Subtle Grid in Stelios Pupet's Contemplative Scenes

Colossal

Athens-based Stelios Pupet starts his murals and canvases from a quiet grid, then lets soft, organic shapes bend and wander away from it. Recent walls include one at an old Columbia records factory in Athens and another in Eberbach, Germany.

Why it's cool: calm, slow paintings — exactly the right mood for a second cup.

See the World's Tallest Day of the Dead Skeleton, Made From Steel, Fiberglass, Papier-Mâché and 70,000 Marigolds

Smithsonian Magazine

For the Day of the Dead, Mexico City raised a La Catrina about 109 feet (33 m) tall, built from steel, fiberglass and papier-mâché and dressed in some 70,000 marigolds — the orange flower said to guide souls home. It beat the previous record for the tallest skeleton of its kind.

Why it's cool: folk art at the scale of a ten-storey building, made of flowers.
