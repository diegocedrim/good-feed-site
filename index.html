🔬 Ciência & Espaço

A rosy stone from the Sahara turns out to be a 4.1-billion-year-old piece of Mars

Smithsonian Magazine

A pinkish rock picked up in the Sahara has been identified as a Martian meteorite roughly 4.1 billion years old — one of the oldest ever found. Rocks this ancient are time capsules from Mars's infancy, and this one carries hints that the young planet may once have been watery.

A fragment of another world's childhood, lying in the desert sand.

A new map of “empty” space challenges a tranquil picture of the galaxy

Quanta Magazine

Researchers combined Gaia's star distances with maps of dust and ultraviolet light to build a 3D picture of the thin gas between stars in our neighborhood. Textbooks say that gas should settle quickly into either a cold or a warm state — but about 41% of it turned out to be in an unstable in-between phase. That constant churning might help explain why galaxies are so surprisingly inefficient at making stars.

Even the “nothing” between the stars is restless.

The asteroid killed the dinosaurs. Why did crocodiles survive?

ScienceDaily

A UCL-led team analyzed more than 7,000 fossils and over 400 species and found that crocodile relatives have followed warm climates for 245 million years. Climate simulations suggest the tropics stayed warm enough after the Chicxulub impact to shelter them, while habitat for non-avian dinosaurs shrank far more. Small bodies, semi-aquatic lives, flexible diets and low energy needs probably helped too.

Sometimes surviving the end of the world is about knowing where the warm spots are.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Corvos tomam decisões duas vezes mais rápido que humanos e macacos

Superinteressante

Num estudo publicado na Science, pesquisadores da Universidade de Tübingen mostraram a gralhas-pretas uma tela com pontinhos se movendo e pediram que bicassem o lado para onde a maioria ia. As aves decidiram em cerca de 235 milissegundos — mais ou menos metade do tempo de humanos e outros primatas — sem perder a precisão. E os circuitos cerebrais envolvidos lembram muito os dos primatas, apesar de cérebros tão diferentes.

Um cérebro do tamanho de uma noz, decidindo mais rápido que o seu.

Aztec methods and crocodiles: how biologists are restoring mangroves in Mexico

Mongabay

In Veracruz's Alvarado lagoons, the group REVIVE plants mangrove seedlings on chinampas — the raised mounds the Aztecs farmed around their capital. They have also released about 80 Morelet's crocodiles since 2017, which they believe help keep channels open (independent experts say that part still needs research). From 2,000 initial plantings, the team reports more than 4,000 trees and 80% canopy cover by year eight.

A 600-year-old farming trick, rebuilding a forest at the water's edge.

Livestock guardian dogs enlisted to help Texas' smallest fox

Phys.org

Fewer than 250 swift foxes are thought to remain in Texas, and coyotes are one of their main threats. Researchers from Texas A&M AgriLife and Texas Tech are testing whether the big dogs that already guard cattle on the Rita Blanca grasslands can keep coyotes away — giving the little foxes safe ground. It's a three-year project, and it's early days.

Ranchers' dogs as accidental bodyguards for a tiny wild fox.

Talento técnico brasileiro competindo de igual para igual com o mundo.

Só Notícia Boa

Depois de nove anos servindo nos corredores dos aviões, Tayrine Souza vai passar para a cabine. A promoção foi anunciada pelo próprio comandante durante o último voo dela como comissária, e passageiros e tripulação comemoraram tanto que ela comparou a festa a uma final de Copa do Mundo.

Do corredor para o manche — no mesmo avião.

Two widowed retirees fall in love again at 88, after meeting at the gym

Good News Network

Barbara Thompson started going to the gym at her Birmingham retirement village to keep a friend company — and kept going three times a week when the friend stopped. Fred Jenkins, a former Army PT instructor, began coaching her, and friendship slowly became romance. Now they garden, dance, play darts and travel together, though both happily keep their own flats.

Proof that it's never too late for a new chapter.

🛠️ Tecnologia, História & Curiosidades

A 2,800-year-old bone flute unearthed in Troy

Smithsonian Magazine

After more than 150 years of digging at Troy, archaeologists have found the first musical instrument from its ruins: a flute carved from bone. It dates to roughly the era when Homer is thought to have composed the Iliad and the Odyssey.

You can almost hear the music people played while those epics were being sung.

Where is England's oldest pub? Tree rings may have the answer

Smithsonian Magazine

Many English pubs claim to be the oldest, so an archaeologist drilled into the 600-year-old timbers of a Somerset inn to settle things scientifically. Tree-ring dating suggests it has been serving travelers since the 1430s, when monks built it to lodge wool merchants.

Nearly six centuries of pints, measured one growth ring at a time.

Rediscovered 1935 recordings capture the earliest known voices of Louisiana Creole

Phys.org

In 1935, linguist Lorenzo Dow Turner recorded Creole speakers in Louisiana on aluminum discs, which ended up simply labeled “Louisiana” in an Indiana University archive. Cambridge researcher Oliver Mayeux tracked them down and analyzed them. Today the language has only a few thousand elderly native speakers, and these recordings preserve sounds and variations no written transcript ever could.

Voices from 90 years ago, helping keep a language alive.

🎨 Arte & Design

Timothy Boomer captures the uncanny world of insect galls

Colossal

Tiny gall wasps trick plants into growing strange, colorful little structures that shelter and feed their larvae — and there are at least 1,300 species of these wasps worldwide. Photographer Timothy Boomer stacks hundreds or thousands of exposures to show the galls in astonishing detail, editing as little as possible because, as he says, nature doesn't need enhancing.

Alien-looking sculptures hiding on ordinary oak leaves.

Livros e leituras nos grafites de São Paulo

Jornal da USP

No livro Nos Muros da Cidade (Ateliê Editorial), a professora Sandra Reimão, da EACH-USP, percorre os grafites paulistanos que falam de leitura: crianças lendo, o direito à arte e à literatura, até a releitura de um mural jesuítico feita por artistas indígenas. Nomes como Alex Vallauri, Rony, Paulo Ito e Sé Cordeiro aparecem nessa galeria a céu aberto.

Um bom motivo para olhar os muros da cidade com mais calma.
