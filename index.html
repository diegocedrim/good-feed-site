How Spiders Spin 'Silk Dope' Into Nature's Toughest Material

Quanta Magazine

Inside a black widow, silk starts out as a liquid "dope" of floppy, unstructured proteins. Gregory Holland's team at San Diego State University found that these proteins first gather into semiliquid droplets, where two amino acids, arginine and tyrosine, act like weak molecular stickers that squeeze out water and line the proteins up.

Those stickers survive all the way into the finished fiber and seem to guide it into its famously strong structure. Lab-made silk still falls short of the real thing, so knowing the recipe step by step matters.

A thread tougher than steel, assembled from what is basically goo.

Saharan dust carries desert bacteria across the Atlantic to Amazon rainforest

Phys.org

Researchers from the University of Graz, the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry and Brazilian partners sampled the air every day for three weeks at the ATTO tower, deep in the Amazon, including five days when Saharan dust arrived. Genetic tests showed that the dust brought bacteria matching those living in African desert soil crusts, and they noticeably changed the mix of microbes floating over the forest.

The fungi in the air barely changed. Whether these travelers arrive alive, and what they do once there, is the next question.

The Amazon breathes a little bit of Africa every summer.

Ghostly Cat's Head Nebula Lurks Within Sight of NASA's Hubble, Webb

NASA

About 7,500 light-years away in the Carina Nebula, a pillar of dust looks remarkably like a sitting cat, complete with a glowing eye. Hubble sees it as a dark silhouette; Webb's infrared eyes see through it, turning it translucent and revealing stars and even distant galaxies behind.

Putting the views side by side helps astronomers work out how stars are born inside clouds like this one.

Halloween week, and the universe delivered a ghost cat.

Smartphones, ancient tracking skills and a future for desert lions

Positive News

In Namibia's Kunene region, the Lion Rangers work across 11 community conservancies, combining smartphones, camera traps and GPS collars with old-fashioned tracking skills. When a lion heads toward a farm, rangers get an early warning and a rapid-response team gently pushes it back.

Fewer clashes between farmers and lions mean fewer lions killed. The population, which was down to about 20 adults in 1997, is now around 60 and considered stable.

High tech and ancestral knowledge, working the same shift.

Fishing cat rescue-and-release offers potential model for conservation in Bangladesh

Mongabay

In February, a male fishing cat was found in northern Bangladesh injured, starving and unable to eat on his own. After months of care, including tube feeding, he reached about 12 kg, spent eight weeks in a soft-release enclosure on a tea estate, and was set free with a satellite collar.

The rescuers hope partnering with tea plantations can become a model in such a crowded country. Other experts note it is not full rewilding yet, which is exactly what the collar data should help clarify.

A cat that fishes for a living, back at work.

The world's newest carnivorous plant is a red sundew that "death hugs" its insect prey

Smithsonian Magazine

Botanists have described a new species, the Florida threadleaf sundew: a slender, bright-red plant whose sticky leaves curl around the insects they catch. It grows only around hidden sinkhole lakes in the Florida Panhandle.

That habitat is rare and shrinking, so giving the plant a name is also the first step toward protecting it.

Even in a well-mapped place like Florida, new species are still waiting to be noticed.

Romans made color-changing glass, and we've just replicated it after 1,600 years

Good News Network

The Lycurgus Cup in the British Museum looks jade green when light bounces off it and glows ruby red when light shines through it. The trick is tiny nanoparticles of gold and silver in the glass. Lisa Hollands (University of Sheffield) and Ian Freestone (UCL) set out to recreate it using only materials Romans had.

The key turned out to be oxygen levels, temperature and even where the glass sat in the furnace. Glass artist Elliot Walker then made a homage piece that will be shown permanently at Sheffield's Turner Museum of Glass.

Roman craftsmen were doing nanotechnology without knowing the word.

The phantom lighthouse: New research explains one of Australia's strangest illusions

Phys.org

At night on Lady Elliot Island, on the Great Barrier Reef, people standing near the lighthouse often "see" the beam coming from a second lighthouse out on the horizon, one that doesn't exist. The real light is behind them, and the illusion holds even when they know it.

Researchers explain that in the dark the brain has very few distance cues, so it guesses. It judges the far end of the beam to be closer and slower than it is, and that motion makes the light seem to come from in front.

Your brain would rather invent a lighthouse than admit it isn't sure.

Did women and children help make China's Terra-Cotta Army? Fingerprints may hold the answer

Smithsonian Magazine

The clay warriors guarding China's first emperor were long assumed to be the work of male craftsmen. But the people who shaped them left fingerprints in the clay 2,000 years ago, and a new study analyzed them.

Most of the prints studied seem to belong to adult women and to children under 15, which suggests whole families may have worked in the workshops.

A tiny fingerprint can rewrite who gets credit for a wonder of the world.

The physics of getting fired

Aeon · Zahaan Bharmal

Fired from his first consulting job in 2001, Zahaan Bharmal did what he had done since a lonely adolescence: he reached for physics. From chaos theory he takes the idea that small shocks can cascade through a system, and from there he arrives at lessons about resilience, which he says comes from spare capacity rather than endless optimization.

His conclusion is a gentle one: physics is most useful not as a source of certainty, but as a humble way of questioning assumptions and learning to live with not knowing.
