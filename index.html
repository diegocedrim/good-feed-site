The sky in Van Gogh's 'Starry Night' matches the predawn sky around July 22, 1889

Phys.org

Researchers in South Korea compared the swirling sky in The Starry Night with what was actually overhead in Saint-Rémy in 1889. Using shape-matching on the painted stars, site visits, Van Gogh's letters and old weather records, they found it fits the predawn sky of about July 22: a waning crescent Moon, Venus, and the Hyades cluster in Taurus. That shifts the usual date by about a month and suggests the painting started from real observation.

Next time you see the painting, you can find Venus in it.

Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

ScienceDaily

Men and women matched for height and clothing walked slowly toward city birds in parks across five European countries, and researchers measured when each bird flew off. Across 2,701 trials and 37 species, from sparrows to magpies, the birds left about a meter earlier when the walker was a woman. Nobody knows why yet: smell, body shape and walking style are all guesses waiting to be tested.

A clear, repeatable result with no explanation. That's where science gets fun.

NASA's Perseverance Mars rover just shattered the all-time off-world driving record

ScienceDaily

On October 11, Perseverance passed 45.2 km of total driving and beat the record Opportunity took almost 15 years to set. Perseverance got there in five and a half years, mostly because its AutoNav system picks its own path across the terrain; it drove more than 90% of the distance on its own. It's now heading for rocks about 4 billion years old at the rim of Jezero Crater, on a mission extended to about 2028.

🌱 Natureza & Animais

Samoan Island Witnesses Explosion of Native Wildlife Following Removal of Invasive Rats and Pigs

Good News Network

Over 12 expeditions between March 2025 and May 2026, conservation teams and local communities removed invasive rats and feral pigs from Nu'utele, an island in Samoa. A year later, native seedlings cover the forest floor, land crabs are back, and birds like the Samoan starling and shy ground dove are nesting where their eggs used to get eaten. The next step is bringing in the threatened Mao, a native honeyeater.

Seabird guano and native trees keep islands above rising seas: Study

Mongabay

A study of 28 atolls in the Indian and Pacific oceans found that rat-free islands with lots of seabirds grow their surrounding reefs up to 1.8 times faster. Bird droppings carry nutrients from the sea onto land and back into the water, feeding corals and fish that make the sand atolls are built from. Native trees hold the soil together, so a healthy island can keep up with rising seas.

Pairs well with the Samoa story: get rid of the rats, and the birds rebuild the island.

These Snakes Can Cartwheel Away From Danger

Smithsonian Magazine

Small snakes in the genus Pseudorabdion have an unusual escape move: they curl into a loop and cartwheel away. Scientists have now worked out how they do it, and found the rolls reach about 6 to 13 km/h, very fast for an animal that size.

A snake that cartwheels. That's enough reason.

Strangers Help 86-Year-Old Who's Been Building a 62-foot Boat in His Yard Since 1987

Good News Network

Since 1987, Dale, from Mineral Wells, Texas, has been building a 19-meter steel boat in his yard, meant to sail to Alaska someday. It's about 75% done, but at 86 he can't finish it alone, and a steep hill stands between the boat and the road. Where Exactly Were the First Humans Captured in a Photograph Standing?

Smithsonian Magazine

Daguerre's view of the Boulevard du Temple in Paris, taken in the late 1830s, is famous for a tiny figure who stood still long enough to show up in the long exposure. Everyone else on the busy street moved too fast to register. Three filmmakers spent two years digging through 19th-century Paris to pin down exactly where he stood, and the spot now has a bronze plaque.

A man getting his shoes shined became, by accident, the first person ever photographed.

Hunebed D27 in Borger, Netherlands

Atlas Obscura

In the Dutch village of Borger stands the largest of the Netherlands' "hunebedden": a burial monument more than 22 meters long and over 5,000 years old, built from huge glacial boulders. Its biggest capstone weighs around 20 tons, and the site has never been fully excavated.

Farmers in the Stone Age moved 20-ton stones without metal tools.

Archaeologists May Have Solved the Mystery of a Roman Sarcophagus Excavated at Westminster Abbey in 1869

Smithsonian Magazine

A Roman stone coffin dug up at Westminster Abbey in 1869 has puzzled historians ever since: why was it there? Researchers now think it was reused in the 10th or 11th century to bury an abbot who ran the early monastery, centuries after it was first carved.

Em tempos de IA, estúdio de Coraline leva 7 anos para criar novo filme à mão, quadro a quadro

Ibrahim Said Nods to Ancient Islamic and Egyptian Pottery Traditions in 'Shaping Time'

Colossal

Ibrahim Said grew up among the pottery workshops of Old Cairo, and he makes white earthenware sculptures of linked vessels covered in fine latticework inspired by medieval Fustat ware. His new show in New York follows an 18-month residency studying the Met's collection. He sees the slow, careful work of shaping clay as a way of working with time, with past and imagined futures layered on top of each other.

She Painted the First Known Self-Portrait of an Artist at Work Nearly 500 Years Ago. Who Was Catharina van Hemessen?

Smithsonian Magazine

In the 1540s, the Flemish painter Catharina van Hemessen painted herself at the easel, brush in hand, and signed it. It's the earliest known self-portrait of an artist actually at work, by a man or a woman. A new exhibition in Antwerp is bringing her largely forgotten career back into view.

Wikipédia

Other people

Aeon · Elizabeth Oldfield · ~30 min

Elizabeth Oldfield and her husband stepped off the usual path (own house, nuclear family) and moved in with six unrelated adults in south London. Her essay is warm and honest about what that's really like: arguing over the washing-up, having to grow up emotionally, and finding that living closely with others wears away the flattering stories we tell ourselves. Drawing on neuroscience, she argues that people keep being shaped by their relationships all their lives, and that a shared community needs a purpose bigger than convenience to last.

Good for a slow coffee, and maybe for calling a friend afterward.
