Scientists may have finally solved the mystery of the Moon's two faces

ScienceDaily

The side of the Moon we see is smooth and dark; the far side is rugged and pale, and nobody knew quite why. A team led by Hejiu Hui at Nanjing University compared the first rocks ever brought back from the far side, collected by China's Chang'e-6, with Apollo and Chang'e-5 samples from the near side.

Deep down, the mantle looks about the same on both sides. The crusts are very different, though. The proposed explanation: once the Moon locked its face toward us, Earth's tides heated it unevenly, setting off huge loops of magma that moved material from one hemisphere to the other.

Earth may have shaped the very face of the Moon we look at every night.

Not all early birds evolved toward better flight, Jurassic fossil suggests

Phys.org

Meet Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis, a crow-sized bird that lived 150 million years ago in what is now Fujian, China. It had serrated teeth and a sickle-shaped claw on its second toe, just like small raptor dinosaurs. It also seems to have lived on the ground and not flown at all.

The team, led by Wang Min of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, published the find in Nature. It suggests the first birds didn't all march toward better flight. Some took detours and tried out very different ways of living.

Evolution isn't a ladder. It's a bush with branches going every which way.

Your dog may link sounds with shapes, just like you do

Phys.org

Say "bouba" and "kiki" and ask people which one is the round blob and which is the spiky star. Almost everyone, in almost every language, gives the same answer. Researchers at the University of Parma tested 70 pet dogs: the dogs watched two screens while one of the made-up words played over and over.

The dogs looked longer at the round shape when they heard "bouba" and at the spiky one when they heard "kiki." In a second round, the owners wore headphones so they couldn't hint at the answer without meaning to. The results appeared in Proceedings of the Royal Society B.

This sense that some sounds are "round" may be much older than human language.

'Patrick' sea star went viral, but Argentine deep-sea canyon held 57 new species

Mongabay

In 2025, the research ship Falkor (too) sent a robot down into Argentina's Mar del Plata Canyon, which drops more than 3,500 meters. Over 4 million people watched the livestream, and a chubby orange sea star that looked just like Patrick from SpongeBob became the internet's favourite.

The real work came this July, when twenty taxonomists from Latin America and Europe spent two weeks in Buenos Aires going through the samples. They described 57 species new to science, including corals, copepods and "Loki," a sea cucumber with horns that lives 2,577 meters down. About 90% of the copepods still haven't even been examined.

There's a lot left to discover just off our continent's coast.

Two rare pregnancies spark hope for Sumatran rhinos

Mongabay

Two females, Ratu and Delilah, are pregnant at the Way Kambas sanctuary in Indonesia, with births expected around May 2027. That matters a lot: only 34 to 47 Sumatran rhinos are left in the wild, and just 11 live in human care anywhere in the world.

If all goes well, Ratu will be the first Sumatran rhino known to have four calves. The article is honest about the catch: the group is so small that every animal is related, so the next challenge is bringing in new genes.

Two calves sounds like nothing, but for this species it's a big deal.

A family of barn owls has moved into Manhattan's Upper West Side

Smithsonian Magazine

Barn owls are extremely rare in New York City, but a whole family has moved into the Upper West Side. It may be the first time the species has nested in Manhattan in modern history.

Barn owls are the ones with the pale, heart-shaped face and a silent flight. They usually hunt over open fields and farmland, so finding a family among Manhattan's buildings is a real surprise.

Even in the most concrete city on Earth, nature finds a gap.

Teenagers worldwide are tackling the plastics crisis

Reasons to be Cheerful

A school in Singapore counted its plastic and found its 2,000 students went through 72,000 items a year. They campaigned for reusable lunch boxes and won the 2026 Unplastify Challenge. Since 2018, more than 3,000 students in 25 countries have joined the program, keeping an estimated 72 tons of plastic out of the bin.

The ideas are wonderfully local. In Colombia, students got butchers and grocers to wrap food in banana leaves. In Costa Rica, they swapped plastic sponges for luffa. In Chile, they made biodegradable glitter. And in Brazil, teenagers worked with uniform makers to cut out polyester.

Fewer than 10% of plastics get recycled. These kids went straight to the source.

Archaeologists in Egypt discover a tomb with a heartbreaking elegy to a father

Smithsonian Magazine

In a mud-brick tomb in the Bir al-Shaghala necropolis, in Egypt, archaeologists found a stone slab carved with a 19-line poem. In it, a son mourns his father.

Specialists call it "a highly literary and original epigram." This isn't a stock funeral formula. Someone sat down and chose each word to say how much they missed their dad.

Thousands of years later, the feeling reads exactly the same.

An unprecedented trove of wooden tools shows Neanderthals were skilled woodworkers

Smithsonian Magazine

Wood almost never survives tens of thousands of years, which is why we mostly picture Neanderthals with stone tools. At the Abric Romaní rock shelter in Spain, though, repeated flooding and minerals preserved nearly 600 wooden objects or their imprints.

At least 13 of them were clearly shaped by hand. It's a rare look at a part of Neanderthal craft that had almost completely vanished from the record.

Our "primitive" cousins were carpenters too.

World's heaviest pumpkin sets incredible new record at 2,856 lbs

Good News Network

Ian Paton, 65, from Lymington in England, grew a pumpkin that weighed 1,295.8 kg at the Wargrave Nursery Giant Pumpkin Weigh-Off. That's heavier than a white rhino and heavier than a VW Beetle.

He named it "Bagheera," after the panther in Kipling's The Jungle Book. It drank about 500 liters of water a day and got six hours of care daily. Last year's record belonged to Ian and his brother Stuart together. This was his first try on his own.

Imagine the size of the pumpkin pie.

221 camera obscuras fill Pneuhaus' immersive, inflatable installation

Colossal

Rhode Island studio Pneuhaus built a white, inflatable structure of more than 65 m², somewhere between an igloo and a giant snail. Its surface is dotted with 221 tiny lenses, and each one works as a camera obscura, projecting the sky, trees and buildings outside onto the soft walls inside.

It runs only on sunlight and records nothing. Visitors can press on the walls to bend the images, like walking around inside an insect's compound eye. The piece premiered at the MODE photography festival.

Photography at its oldest, with no screen and no file.

'Arcana Borealis' summons the power of spirituality and artist communities

Colossal

Hilma af Klint was painting abstract works in Stockholm around 1906, about four years before Kandinsky, who usually gets the credit. A new show at Helsinki's Ateneum, opening October 23 and running until February 2027, brings together more than 200 works by 50 Nordic women who drew on spirituality, meditation and the esoteric.

Instead of telling the story of a lone genius, the exhibition looks at groups such as The Five, af Klint's circle of friends. It spans painting, textiles, sculpture and video, from Finland and Sweden to Iceland and Sápmi.

Wikipédia

Num jardim de Roundhay, em Leeds, na Inglaterra, o inventor francês Louis Le Prince filmou alguns parentes e amigos andando em círculos e rindo. São só uns dois segundos de imagem, mas a Roundhay Garden Scene é considerada o filme mais antigo que chegou até nós. Isso foi sete anos antes da famosa sessão dos irmãos Lumière.

Triplet of 'Ghostly' Galaxies Hints at What Dark Matter Is — and Isn't

Quanta Magazine

Some 65 million light-years away sit three faint, see-through galaxies (DF2, DF4 and DF9) that seem to hold almost no dark matter. That's odd, because nearly every galaxy is wrapped in a lot of it. A Yale team led by Michael Keim has a theory: long ago, two galaxies crashed into each other. The dark matter passed right through, and the ordinary gas and stars clumped together into these "ghosts."

The twist is the best part. If dark matter can be separated from normal matter, it's probably something real, and not just a sign that gravity works differently, as rival theories like MOND claim. As one researcher puts it, the absence of dark matter is, in a way, evidence that it exists. About 10 minutes of reading, a good fit for a second coffee.
